# Candlesticks: What They Are and How to Read Them

> **Midas Academy · Phase 1 · Module 2 Candlestick Basics**

**Quick Overview**

- A Candlestick (Candle Chart) uses **one candle to record a period of price**, serving as the foundation for understanding the market.
- Each candlestick contains **Four Prices**: Open, Close, High, and Low.
- The **Body** is formed by the Open and Close prices; the **Wick** is the thin line connecting the body to the High/Low prices (Upper Wick, Lower Wick).
- **Bullish Candle** (Close > Open, Red in Midas) has the lower edge as the Open and the upper edge as the Close; **Bearish Candle** (Close < Open, Green in Midas) is the opposite. Note that color conventions may differ across markets.
- **Period** determines how long a candle represents; there is no "good" or "bad" period—choose based on your observation goal.

## I. Concept Introduction: One Candle, One Price Story

A Candlestick (also known as a Candle Chart) is the most common form of price chart in the market. It looks like a candle—thick in the middle, with thin "wicks" extending up and down.

This method originated in the Japanese rice market centuries ago, where merchants used it to record rice price fluctuations (attributed to the rice merchant Munehisa Homma of the Edo period, later introduced to modern financial markets and still in use today). It has remained popular for so long because **one candlestick can clearly tell the story of "what happened to price over a period of time"**: how much it opened at, how much it closed at, how high it surged, and how low it dipped—all visible at a glance.

Learning to read candlesticks is the first step in understanding the market. The technical indicators, patterns, and **Chan Theory** discussed later are all built upon candlesticks.

## II. The Four Prices of a Candlestick

Every candlestick condenses four key prices over a period of time, collectively known as the "Four Prices":

- **Open Price**: The first transaction price at the **start** of this period.
- **Close Price**: The last transaction price at the **end** of this period.
- **High Price**: The **highest point** the price reached during this period.
- **Low Price**: The **lowest point** the price reached during this period.

Remember these four prices, and you have mastered the complete source of information for a candlestick—the remaining "Body," "Wick," and "Color" are merely ways of visualizing these four prices.

## III. Body and Wick: How Four Prices Become a Candle

Placing these four prices on a chart forms the two parts of a candlestick:

**Body (The thick part)**: Formed by the **Open and Close prices**. The distance between the Open and Close prices is the height of the body.

**Wick (The thin part)**: The thin lines extending above and below the body.
- The thin line from the **top of the body** to the High price is called the **Upper Wick**—representing that the price once surged to this height.
- The thin line from the **bottom of the body** to the Low price is called the **Lower Wick**—representing that the price once dipped to this low.

Simply put: **The Body records "Open to Close," and the Wick records "Extreme highs and lows reached."**

## IV. Bullish and Bearish Candles: Color Represents Rise or Fall

At the end of a period, the price is either higher than the Open or lower than the Open, distinguishing two types of candlesticks:

- **Bullish Candle**: Close Price **Higher** than Open Price (the period was an uptrend). In Midas (following A-share conventions), the Bullish Candle is **Red**. In this case, the **lower edge of the body is the Open, and the upper edge is the Close**.
- **Bearish Candle**: Close Price **Lower** than Open Price (the period was a downtrend). In Midas, the Bearish Candle is **Green**. In this case, the **upper edge of the body is the Open, and the lower edge is the Close**.

⚠️ **A common pitfall for beginners here:** **A-share conventions are "Red for Rise, Green for Fall," but many overseas markets (such as US stocks, and most cryptocurrency platforms) follow "Green for Rise, Red for Fall," which is exactly the opposite.** Confirm the color rules of the platform before looking at the chart to avoid misinterpreting the colors.

## V. Different Periods: Viewing the Same Market Movement with Different "Scales"

A key attribute of candlesticks is **Period**, which refers to "how long a candle represents."

- **1 Minute Candle**: Represents 1 minute, suitable for observing short-term subtle fluctuations.
- **1 Hour Candle**: Represents 1 hour.
- **Daily Candle**: Represents one trading day.
- There are also Weekly, Monthly, and shorter timeframes like 5-minute and 15-minute.

Viewing the same market movement with different periods presents a completely different "granularity": a plain Bullish candle on a daily chart might appear as hundreds of small candlesticks flipping up and down on a 1-minute chart. **There is no "good" or "bad" period, only what suits your observation goal**—use large periods for the big picture and small periods for details.

## VI. Connecting Multiple Candles: The Trajectory of Price

A single candlestick tells the story of "a period of time." When arranged in chronological order, many candlesticks form the price trend charts we see daily.

Through the sequence of candlesticks, you can intuitively see: is the price rising steadily (a series of Bullish candles with an elevated center of gravity), oscillating (switching between Red and Green with a flat center of gravity), or falling? This serves as the canvas for learning "Trends" and "Patterns" later on.

# Risk-Reward Ratio: Why You Can't Just Look at Win Rate

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 13 Trading System and Practical Combat

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Risk–Reward Ratio (profit and loss ratio) = potential profit ÷ potential loss**, determined by entry, Stop Loss, and Take Profit.
- **Looking only at the winning rate will lead to misjudgment**: a winning rate of 40% + a profit-loss ratio of 1:2 may make a profit; a winning rate of 70% but a poor profit-loss ratio (making less profit and losing more) may make a loss.
- The profit-loss ratio and the winning rate should be considered together: the higher the profit-loss ratio, the lower the required break-even winning rate (1:1 → about 50%, 1:2 → about 33%, 1:3 → about 25%); it is common to do at least 1:2.
- ⚠️ This is the concept of **mathematical expectation**, based on multi-Stroke statistics, **does not guarantee single Stroke results, does not predict, and does not guarantee profits**.

## 1. Concept introduction: Does a high winning rate mean you will definitely make money?

Many novices are obsessed with "win rate" - always looking for a way to "get it right eight times out of ten". But there is a counter-intuitive but extremely important fact in trading: just looking at the winning rate, you cannot judge whether a method can make money at all. **

A guy with a 70% win rate might be losing money, and a guy with a 40% win rate might be making money. What's the difference? Another overlooked key - **Risk–Reward Ratio (profit and loss ratio)**. This article will explain it thoroughly.

## 2. What is Risk–Reward Ratio?

**Risk–Reward Ratio, which measures "how much you can potentially earn" versus "how much you can potentially lose" from a Stroke transaction. **

Simply put: **Profit/Loss Ratio = Potential Profit ÷ Potential Loss. **

It is usually written in the form of "risk:reward", such as **Risk–Reward Ratio 1:2**, which means: you take 1 risk (potential loss 1) and gamble 2 rewards (potential profit 2). In a Stroke transaction, it is determined by your **entry price, Stop Loss level, and Take Profit target** - entry from Stop Loss is "risk", and entry to Take Profit is "reward".

## 3. Why profit-loss ratio is more important than winning rate

The key is: the profit and loss of ** trading is determined by "how much you make when you win" and "how much you lose when you lose", not just "the number of wins". **

Give two examples (the numbers are just to illustrate the principle):

- **Example A: The win rate is only 40%, but the Risk–Reward Ratio is 1:2. **
  Do 10 strokes and take 1 risk for each stroke: if you win 4 strokes, you will earn 2 → +8 for each stroke; if you lose 6 strokes, you will lose 1 → −6 for each stroke. Total **+2**. **The winning rate is less than half, but overall it is profitable. **

- **Example B: The winning rate is as high as 70%, but the Risk–Reward Ratio is very poor (for example, profit 0.3, loss 1). **
  Do 10 Strokes: Win 7 Strokes, earn 0.3 → +2.1 per Stroke; lose 3 Strokes, lose 1 → −3 per Stroke. Total **−0.9**. **The winning rate is very high, but the overall result is a loss. **

Example B is a true portrayal of many people: **Run away after making a little profit (Take Profit too early), and bear the loss (Stop Loss too late or even not Stop Loss)**. The winning rate looks good, but the profit-loss ratio is a mess, and long-term losses are inevitable.

## 4. The profit-loss ratio and winning rate should be looked at together: the break-even point

Winning rate and profit-loss ratio are actually a pair of partners that need to work together. Given a profit-loss ratio, there is a "break-even winning rate" - only a winning rate higher than it can be positive in the long run:

- Risk–Reward Ratio **1:1** → The break-even winning rate is about **50%** (you will only make money if it is higher than 50%).
- Risk–Reward Ratio **1:2** → The break-even win rate is about **33%** (anything above 33% is possible).
- Risk–Reward Ratio **1:3** → Breakeven win rate is about **25%**.

It can be seen that: the higher the profit-loss ratio, the lower the requirement for winning rate. ** This is why many mature traders would rather sacrifice some winning rate to ensure a high enough profit and loss ratio - a common approach is to **only do opportunities with a Risk–Reward Ratio of at least 1:2**.

## 5. Boundaries that must be clarified

This set of logic is very powerful, but there are several boundaries that must be explained clearly:

1. **This is a "mathematical expectation" concept, based on the statistics of multi-Stroke transactions and does not guarantee any single-Stroke result. ** The profit-loss ratio is 1:2 and the winning rate is 40%. It is expected to be positive under "long-term and enough times", but **it is entirely possible to lose money continuously** in a certain Stroke or a certain period of time.
2. **Profit-loss ratio and winning rate cannot be pursued in isolation. ** Blindly pursuing an ultra-high profit-loss ratio (the goal of Take Profit is set very far) will often lower the winning rate; what is needed is that the overall expectation after the two are combined is positive**.
3. **It does not predict or guarantee profits. ** It is a framework to help you understand "whether a method will make money or lose money in the long run". It is not a guaranteed profit formula, nor can it predict the winning or losing of the next Stroke. Real transactions are also subject to slippage, handling fees, execution deviations, etc.

# Hedging with Contracts: Protecting Existing Positions

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types (Contract Practice)

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Hedging / Hedging = Offsetting (part of) the risk of an existing position with a position in the opposite direction**, like buying "insurance" for the position.
- Basic structure: **Spot long + contract short order** - When the price falls, the short order's floating profit is hedged against the spot's floating loss (protection), and when the price rises, the profit is also hedged (price); the essence is to "lock in the fluctuation".
- ⚠️ **Cost of hedging**: capital fee cost, **opportunity cost (forgoing rising profits)**, imperfect hedging (basis/mismatch residual), **leverage/Liquidation risk of the contract leg (Liquidation will invalidate the insurance)**.
- ⚠️ Red line: **Hedging reduces directional risk and does not eliminate all risks**; hedging has costs and is not perfect; this is an introduction to the concept, does not instigate complex operations, does not constitute advice, does not guarantee profit, and does not predict**.

## 1. Concept introduction: What should I do if I want to hold it for the long term but are afraid of short-term decline?

Suppose you are optimistic about an asset for a long time and want to hold its spot, but you are worried that there may be a major correction in the future - sell it directly, for fear of selling out and short-term the long-term market; don't sell it, and you will have to watch the correction eat up the floating profit.

Is there any way to not only retain the spot position, but also provide it with "insurance" to block the risk of short-term decline?

This is the basic idea of ​​**Hedge/Hedging**. This article explains its concept, how to use contracts to insure spot goods, and - what must be explained clearly - the cost of hedging, and why hedging reduces directional risk rather than eliminating all risks**.

## 2. What is hedging/hedging?

**Hedging is to use a position in the opposite direction to offset (part of) the risk of an existing position. **

The most appropriate analogy is: **insurance**.

- You buy insurance for your house and **pay a Stroke premium** in exchange for the protection of "compensation in case of fire";
- You hedge your spot position, **paying some cost**, in exchange for the protection of "hedging losses in the event of a decline."

Hedging (hedging) is the application of this idea of ​​"locking risks with reverse positions" in positioning. Its purpose is not to "make more money", but to "control fluctuations and avoid risks in a specific direction".

## 3. Basic idea: long spot + short contract

The most basic hedging structure is: **While holding a long spot position, open a short contract order of the same amount. **

Let’s look at its effect under different market conditions:

- **Price falls**: Spot longs **floating losses**, but contract shorts **floating profits** - the two directions are opposite and offset each other, and the **falling losses are (partially) hedged**. This is exactly the "insurance" you want.
- **Price Rise**: Spot longs **floating profits**, but contract shorts **floating losses** - the rising profits are also hedged.

The net effect is: **The directional fluctuations during this period are "locked in" - there will be neither big losses nor big profits. **

This is the essence of hedging and its "premium": **You "give up rising profits" in exchange for "falling protection." ** Insurance is not free - the price is that you also give up the upside during that period.

## 4. The logic of two-way positions

"Being long (spot) and short (contract) at the same time" sounds contradictory - bullish and bearish at the same time? In fact, its logic is not "betting on both the ups and downs", but "locking in":

- Hedge and lock-in the directional risk of spot positions during this period with short contract orders;
- Suitable for the scenario of "** long-term optimism, but want to avoid volatility in the short term**" - you don't want to sell the long-term position (retain the long-term optimism), but want to temporarily shield the short-term downside risk.

It's critical to understand this: **Two-way positions are a "risk management action" here, not a "directional bet." **

## 5. ★The cost of hedging (must be explained clearly)

Hedging is not a "free lunch", nor is it "elimination of risk". It has clear costs and limitations, which must be made clear:

**1. Capital fee cost. **
Contract short orders are charged according to the Funding Rate (review E2): short positions are charged at a positive rate (this is beneficial to you), but short positions are charged at a negative rate (this becomes your cost). Holding a hedging position means that you have to continue to bear the impact of funding fees-**hedging has a "carrying cost"**.

**2. Opportunity cost. **
This is the biggest "premium" for hedging: **It not only hedges against the downside risk, but also hedges against the upside returns. ** If the price ** rises sharply ** during this period, your spot profit will be offset by the floating loss of the short contract - ** you will not make money from this rise **. Protection comes at a price, and the price is giving up potential upside.

**3. Hedging is not perfect. **
In reality, it is difficult for spot and contract prices to be completely equal and synchronized:

- There is a **basis** between the two prices, and the basis fluctuates (recall E7), causing the hedging to not be 100% accurate;
- Slight mismatches in quantity and timing will also leave some risk in hedging**.

The so-called "complete lock-in" is just an ideal, and there are always residuals in reality.

**4. Leverage/Liquidation risk of contract legs. **
If your contract short order uses **leverage** and does not leave enough margin, then when the price **surges**, the short order may **Liquidation** (Review E4: The liquidation price of the short order is above).

> ⚠️ Once the contract is short Liquidation, your hedging structure will be broken - leaving only unprotected spot long positions, which is equivalent to "insurance failure". Therefore, the contract legs used for hedging must also manage margins and control leverage**, otherwise the "insurance" itself will be in trouble first.

## 6. Red line: Hedging reduces directional risk, but does not eliminate all risks.

Sum up the above costs into the most important sentence:

**What hedging reduces is "directional risk" (the loss caused by the price moving in a certain direction); it cannot eliminate "all risks". **

- It **has a cost** (funding fee + opportunity cost);
- It is **not perfect** (basis differences and mismatches bring residuals);
- Its contract leg** itself also has leverage/Liquidation risk**;
- What it gets in exchange is "volatility is locked in", and the price is giving up rising profits.

It is important to emphasize: **This article is an introduction to concepts, not to instruct you to do complex hedging operations. ** Real hedging involves professional content such as calculation of hedging ratio, dynamic management of margin, rebalancing, etc., which is far more complicated than the phrase "long spot + short contract". Novices only need to understand the principles, and they should not practice them without fully understanding them.

# Long and Short: Making Money in Both Directions

> Midas Academy · Phase 1 · Module 4: Basic Trading Concepts

**Quick Overview**

- Markets allow for **profit in both directions**: Long for rising prices, Short for falling prices.
- **Long**: Buy first, sell later. Profit if price rises, lose if it falls.
- **Short**: Sell first, buy later (analogous to "borrowing and selling, then buying back when price drops"). Profit if price falls, lose if it rises; in contracts, this is achieved by "opening a short position," no need to actually borrow the physical asset.
- **Spot** trading usually focuses on **Long** because it requires holding/borrowing first; **Contracts** are designed to support both directions, making it easy to Long or Short.
- **Long** and **Short** have **opposite profit/loss directions**; you must prepare for "being wrong" in any direction (risk management).

## I. Concept Introduction: There's More Than One Way to Make Money

Many beginners' first impression of trading is: "Buy, then wait for it to rise, and sell to make a profit when it does." This is correct, but it is only half the story.

In the market, prices go up and down. If you can only make money when prices rise, then half of the market movement (the decline) is left unexploited. Therefore, the market has designed two opposite directions for play:

- **Long**: Betting that the price will **rise**.
- **Short**: Betting that the price will **fall**.

These two directions are opposite, but both can be profitable—the difference lies in the direction you judge and how the price actually moves. **Note: This section explains how the two tools/directions operate, not how to predict price movements, nor does it claim that one direction is guaranteed to win.**

## II. Long: Buy Low, Sell High, the Most Intuitive Direction

**Long** is the most intuitive type of trading: **Buy when you think the price is low, and sell after the price rises, capturing the difference.**

- Price rises as expected → Sell price is higher than buy price → **Profit**.
- Price falls as expected → Sell price is lower than buy price → **Loss**.

In short: **Long is "Buy first, sell later," and you only profit if the price rises.**

## III. Short: Sell First, Buy Later, Profit When Price Falls

**Short** is the concept most likely to confuse beginners, so let's use a relatable analogy to clarify.

Assume you **borrow** a camera from a friend (you do not own the camera, you are just borrowing it). The current market price is 10,000. You judge it will drop in price, so you immediately sell it for 10,000 and receive 10,000 in cash. After a while, the price of this camera indeed drops to 8,000. You spend 8,000 to buy back an **identical** one to return to your friend. One borrow, one return, the camera is returned to its owner, and you have **netted a profit of 2,000**.

The logic of Short is exactly this: **Sell at a high price (borrow and sell), then buy back at a low price when the price drops, capturing the difference from the decline.**

- Price falls as expected → Buy-back price is lower than sell price → **Profit**.
- Price rises as expected → Buy-back price is higher than sell price → **Loss**.

In short: **Short is "Sell first, buy later," and you only profit if the price falls.**

(Note: In contract trading, you do not need to actually "borrow" a coin or a stock. You simply "open a short position," and the system calculates your profit/loss based on "profit if it falls, loss if it rises"—"borrowing and selling" is an analogy to help you understand the principle of Shorting.)

## IV. Why Contracts Allow Shorting, While Spot Usually Only Allows Longing

This starts with "whether you actually possess the asset."

**Spot Trading**: You use money to **buy and truly hold** that asset (e.g., buying and holding a specific stock or coin). You own it, so naturally you can sell it to profit when the price rises. However, if you want to Short—selling "first" without holding—you must first **borrow** that asset to sell. Such channels for borrowing stocks or coins are often not open or are complex for ordinary retail investors. Therefore, **Spot trading usually focuses on Long**.

**Contract Trading** (e.g., Perpetual, Futures): A contract is essentially an "agreement on price movement," and it is **designed to support both directions**. You can open a "Long contract" (profit if price rises) or a "Short contract" (profit if price falls), without needing to actually borrow the physical asset. Therefore, **Contracts allow for convenient Longing and Shorting**.

## V. Long vs. Short: Profit and Loss Directions Are Exactly Opposite

When comparing the two, remember this set of symmetric relationships:

| | Long | Short |
|---|---|---|
| View | Bullish | Bearish |
| Operation Order | Buy first, sell later | Sell first, buy later |
| Price Rises | Profit | Loss |
| Price Falls | Loss | Profit |

They are like the two sides of a coin: **For the same market movement, the Long and Short traders have opposite profit and loss.**

Because of this, regardless of which direction you use, you must be prepared for "what happens if you are wrong"—this leads to the risk management (such as Stop Loss) to be covered later. Shorting requires special attention: theoretically, the price has no upper limit when rising, so **the potential loss for Shorting when the direction is wrong can be very large**. This will be covered specifically in the later sections on Contracts and Risk Management.

## VI. Key Takeaways

- Markets allow for **profit in both directions**: Long for rising prices, Short for falling prices.
- **Long**: Buy first, sell later. Profit if price rises, lose if it falls.
- **Short**: Sell first, buy later (analogous to "borrowing and selling, then buying back when price drops"). Profit if price falls, lose if it rises; in contracts, this is achieved by "opening a short position," no need to actually borrow the physical asset.
- **Spot** trading usually focuses on Long because it requires holding/borrowing first; **Contracts** are designed to support both directions, making it easy to Long or Short.
- **Long** and **Short** have **opposite profit/loss directions**; you must prepare for "being wrong" in any direction (risk management).

#Mean reversion strategy: price returns to normal

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Mean reversion = the strategic perspective of "after the price deviates excessively from the mean, it statistically tends to regress" (quantitatively looking at the "degree of deviation", which is different from F9's qualitative operation at both ends of the interval).
- It has an **opposite world view** to trend following (betting on regression vs. betting on continuation), which are suitable for shock/trend markets respectively.
- Tools for measuring deviations: **Moving Average deviation, Bollinger Bands (deviation from the upper and lower rails), RSI overbought and oversold**; enter the market against the trend, return to the mean Take Profit, and strictly stop Loss if the deviation continues.
- ⚠️ **Fatal premise: The market must indeed be oscillating**; once it is a trend, if you do mean reversion against the trend, you will be **crushed all the way** (the trend is started and you are trapped); **The price may continue to deviate, and there is no guarantee of return** (overbought can become even more overbought).
- ⚠️ Red line: It is a **high-risk counter-trend strategy**. It is most afraid of the trend starting, and it is easier to liquidate under leverage; **No return is guaranteed, no profit is guaranteed, and strict Stop Loss and risk management are required**. What worked in the past does not mean it will be effective in the future.

## 1. Concept introduction: look at "sell high, buy low" from another perspective

In F9's "Shock Trading System", we talked about selling high and buying low during shocks: buy near support and sell near resistance. That is a **qualitative** operating idea based on "both ends of the interval".

In this article, we look at the same type of behavior from a more **statistical/quantitative** perspective - **Mean Reversion**: treating "prices tend to return to their average levels" as a rule that can be quantitatively measured.

The difference between it and F9 is:

- F9 is to **qualitatively** see "whether the price reaches the upper edge or the lower edge of the range";
- F14 is a **quantitative** look at "how far the price deviates from its mean" - using tools such as deviation rate and standard deviation to measure the "degree of deviation" and judge the possibility of regression accordingly.

This perspective is closer to "strategic and mathematical" thinking, but - it has a fatal premise, which will be emphasized repeatedly in this article.

## 2. What is mean regression?

**Mean reversion is a statistical concept: prices will fluctuate around a certain "mean/normal". When it deviates excessively from the mean, it statistically tends to return to near the mean. **

To use an analogy: Like a small ball attached to a rubber band, the farther it is pulled from the center, the stronger the "returning force" seems to be when it is pulled back to the center. The mean reversion strategy is betting on this "regression after excessive deviation".

**But please remember one word immediately: tendency. ** is "tended to return", ** is not "inevitable to return"**. This distinction is the key to this entire article, and I will return to it repeatedly later.

## 3. Mean reversion vs trend following: two opposite worldviews

Putting mean reversion and the trend following (F12) learned above together, you will find that they are **two completely opposite world views**:

- **Trend following** bets on "**deviation will continue**": the price breaks through and moves in one direction. It believes that this direction will continue, so it follows the trend.
- **Mean Reversion** bets on "**deviation will return**": the price deviates excessively from the mean. It believes that the deviation is too excessive and will be pulled back, so it goes against the trend.

One goes with the trend and the other goes against the trend; one bets on continuation and the other bets on return. **They are suitable for opposite markets respectively** - trend following is suitable for trending markets, and mean reversion is suitable for volatile markets. This once again confirms what F11 said: There is no "more correct" world view, only whether it is suitable for the current market.

## 4. How to measure "how much you deviate"

The "quantification" of mean reversion is reflected in its use of tools to measure "the extent to which prices deviate from the mean." There are three commonly used categories (all indicators learned previously):

**1. Moving Average deviation (deviation rate). **
The distance between the price and a certain Moving Average is the "deviation". **When the price is too far away from the Moving Average (the deviation is too large)**, mean reversion believes that it tends to "pull back" to near the Moving Average. Moving Average represents the "average cost/normality" for a period of time. If the price deviates too much, there will be a statistical tendency of regression.

**2. Bollinger Bands (off track). **
Recall B7/B8: The upper and lower rails of Bollinger Bands = middle rail ± standard deviation, which itself is drawn by "how many standard deviations away from the mean". **When the price touches or even exceeds the upper rail (deviates above) or the lower rail (deviates below)**, in a volatile market, mean reversion expects it to return to near the middle rail (mean).

**3. RSI is overbought or oversold. **
Recall B9: RSI > 70 (overbought), < 30 (oversold), which can be used as a reference for "excessive short-term price deviation". Mean reversion regards overbought as one of the signals that "the price has risen too much and is inclined to fall", and oversold is regarded as "the price has fallen too far and is inclined to rebound".

## 5. Entry and exit logic

Typical entry and exit of mean reversion strategy:

- **Entry (countertrend)**: When the price deviates from the mean to a certain extent (deviates too much/touches the lower track/RSI is oversold), **reverse layout** - such as buying at an oversold position in anticipation of its return.
- **Take Profit**: When the price **returns to near the mean** (such as returning to Moving Average, returning to the middle track of Bollinger Bands), exit and make a profit. What makes money is the period of "from deviation to return".
- **Stop Loss (a matter of life and death)**: When the price** not only does not return, but continues to expand in the deviation direction** (such as falling below the range and the trend starts), **Stop Loss** must be strictly enforced.

Why is this Stop Loss "a matter of life and death"? Because it is directly connected to the fatal premise of mean reversion——

## 6. ★Fatal premise: The market must "really be shaking"

This section is the core of the entire article and the biggest red line of mean reversion:

**For mean reversion to be established, there is a fatal premise - the market must indeed be in a state of shock. Once a market enters a trend, mean reversion can turn into a disaster. **

Why?

**1. In a trend, the price will continue to deviate from the mean and will not return. **
In an upward trend, the price will continue to hit new highs, continue to run above the Moving Average, and be "overbought" for a long time - "deviation" is precisely the normal characteristic of the trend, not a signal of return. At this time, if you follow the logic of mean reversion and "Go short overbought, buy the dip and fall", you are going against the trend and taking the knife.

**2. If you do mean reversion against the trend, you will be crushed by the trend. **
This is exactly what F9 said about "Getting trapped when the trend starts": You thought that the price would fall back after it hit the upper track, so you went short; as a result, the price did not fluctuate, but really broke through upward, and the trend started - you stood in the opposite direction of the trend, and you got trapped deeper and deeper. If you didn't Stop Loss, you would be crushed by a wave of trending market. In the same way as buying the bottom in a downward trend (buying when it is oversold), you will lose more and more along the way.

**3. "The price may continue to deviate and its return is not guaranteed."**
Let me say that key word again: mean reversion is a "tendency", not an "inevitability". **Overbought can become even more overbought, and oversold can become even more oversold** (passivated); there is a loophole in the rubber band metaphor - with real prices, the "rubber band" may be broken (the trend starts), and it will not always bounce back.

**So, mean reversion is essentially a counter-trend strategy with high risks, and the biggest fear is that the trend will start. ** The premise of using it is to first try to confirm that "it is indeed a shock, not a trend", and ** once the price continues to deviate and breaks through the range, immediately stop the loss** - you must not hold on to the illusion that "it will return sooner or later". This "death-carrying fantasy" is the source of big money losses due to mean reversion.

## 7. Applicability and risks

**Suitable market**: **Clear shock range**. When the price fluctuates repeatedly within a range and has no direction, the "deviation → regression" of mean reversion has a statistical basis.

**Unsuitable Quotes/Fatal Risks**: **Trend Quotes**. If you do mean reversion against the trend during the trend, you will be crushed all the way (as in the previous section). This is the opposite weakness of trend following.

**Other Risks**:

- **Judging "shock vs. trend" is inherently difficult and has a lag** - What you think is a shock may be the beginning of a trend.
- ⚠️ **Leverage amplifies risk**: Counter-trend strategies are inherently risky. If leverage is added, the reverse trend will lead to **faster liquidation** when the trend starts. Mean reversion + high leverage is a very dangerous combination.

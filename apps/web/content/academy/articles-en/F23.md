# Contract Position Sizing: The 1% Risk Rule Under Leverage

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types (Contract Practice)

**Quick overview of this article**

- ★**Nominal exposure (= margin × leverage) ≠ Margin occupation**; **Profit and loss are calculated based on nominal exposure**, not based on margin ("Only investing 1,000 will result in a maximum loss of 1,000" is a fatal misunderstanding).
- **1% risk method is applied to the contract**: the maximum loss for a single Stroke is 1% → **Nominal exposure = risk amount ÷ Stop Loss range**; **Leverage only determines the margin occupation, not the loss of a single Stroke**; determine the risk first and then reverse the leverage, rather than full leverage first.
- ★**Anti-Liquidation**: **Stop Loss price must be triggered before Liquidation price**; the higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price will be. **Excessive leverage will cause "Liquidation before Stop Loss"**, and the 1% risk method will be invalid; low enough leverage must be used to ensure that the Liquidation price is far from the Stop Loss price.
- Complete logical chain: Set 1% risk → Calculate exposure → Choose low leverage (Liquidation price is far higher than Stop Loss) → Set margin → Strict Stop Loss.
- ⚠️ Red line: The higher the leverage, the closer it is to Liquidation, and the more light the position (low leverage) is; **Survival is prioritized over making more**; no profit is guaranteed, no prediction is made, the price is a logical indication, not a buying and selling signal.

## 1. Concept introduction: Can the "1% risk method" still be used in contracts?

F4 talked about the **fixed proportion risk method** of spot: each Stroke transaction requires a maximum loss of a fixed small proportion (such as 1%) of the total funds. It is the core lesson of "survival".

Will this still work if I move it into a contract? **It works, but you must first understand a key difference that has been miscalculated by countless novices-"position value (notional exposure)" and "margin occupation" are not the same thing at all. ** If you don't understand this, the "risk" you calculate in the contract will be ridiculously wrong.

In this article, the 1% risk method is correctly applied to the contract and connected with the anti-Liquidation of E4/E9.

## 2. ★Key difference: nominal exposure vs margin occupation

This is the first hurdle in contract risk management. Two concepts must be distinguished:

- **Margin (occupied)**: Your **actual deposit** - the money in your account occupied by this Stroke position.
- **Nominal exposure (position value)**: The size of the position you **actually control** = **Margin × Leverage**.

Example: **1,000 yuan margin × 10 times leverage = 10,000 yuan nominal exposure. **

**Here comes the key: your profit and loss is calculated based on [nominal exposure of 10,000 yuan], not [margin of 1,000 yuan]. **

- If the price fluctuates by 1%, profit and loss = 10,000 × 1% = 100 yuan (relative to 1,000 margin, it is 10%).
- This is the essence of leverage "amplifying profit and loss": profit and loss are anchored to the **magnified exposure**.

> ⚠️ The most fatal misunderstanding among novices: "I only invested 1,000 as a deposit, and the most I can lose is 1,000." - Wrong. The risk should be calculated based on the **nominal exposure** and the **Stop Loss amplitude**; and under 10 times leverage, if the exposure of 10,000 yuan only has a **reverse fluctuation of 10% (1,000 yuan)**, the margin will be liquidated (Liquidation). You thought you only bet 1,000, but you actually took on a price risk of 10,000.

## 3. Correctly apply the 1% risk method to the contract

Once you understand the difference between exposure and margin, you can correctly apply the 1% risk method. The core logic is exactly the same as F4, except that the "exposure" quantity must be used correctly.

**Step one: The maximum loss for the order Stroke (1%). **
The total capital is 100,000 yuan, and the risk of a single Stroke is set to 1% → the maximum loss for a single Stroke is **1,000 yuan**.

**Step 2: Use the Stop Loss amplitude to infer the nominal exposure (continue to the F4 formula). **

> **Nominal exposure (position value) = Single Stroke risk amount ÷ Stop Loss range (%)**

- If the Stop Loss range is 5%: Nominal exposure = 1,000 ÷ 5% = **20,000 yuan**.
- Meaning: You open a position with an exposure of 20,000 yuan. If the price reverses by 5% and triggers Stop Loss, the loss = 20,000 × 5% = 1,000 yuan = 1% of the total funds. **Single Stroke loss is locked at 1%. **

**Step 3: Leverage and margin are determined accordingly (not the other way around). **

The nominal exposure is set (20,000 yuan), how much margin is required and how much leverage is used?

- Using **10 times** leverage: Margin = 20,000 ÷ 10 = **2,000 yuan**;
- Use **2 times** leverage: Margin = 20,000 ÷ 2 = **10,000 yuan**.

**Note a key insight: Regardless of whether 10x or 2x leverage is used, the "maximum loss of 1,000 yuan (1%) for a single Stroke" for this Stroke transaction remains unchanged** - because the loss of a single Stroke is determined by [nominal exposure × Stop Loss range] and has been locked. **Leverage changes only how much margin is occupied by this exposure, not how much you lose on a single Stroke. **

![F23 with picture 2](/academy-img/F23-配图2.png)

This subverts the order of operations for many people. The wrong approach is "**choose a high leverage first, and then reposition the shuttle**"; the correct logical chain is:

**First place an order for Stroke risk (1%) → Calculate the nominal exposure based on the Stop Loss range → Determine the margin and leverage accordingly. ** Leverage is a "calculated result", not an "input that is full as soon as it comes up".

## 4. ★Anti-Liquidation: Let Stop Loss always be before Liquidation price

The 1% risk method controls "the loss when you **actively Stop Loss**" (locked at 1%). But there is a premise - **Your Stop Loss price must be triggered before the Liquidation price** (Review E9: Stop Loss is set before the Liquidation price). Otherwise, you will be liquidated before you can Stop Loss.

Here again, leverage is the key variable. Review E4: **The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the position price** (approximately (100÷N)%).

Look at it together with your Stop Loss amplitude (still taking Go Long and Stop Loss amplitude of 5% as an example):

- **Low leverage (e.g. 5x)**: Liquidation price is about 20% in the opposite direction - well outside of your 5% Stop Loss. When the price falls, **hit 5% Stop Loss** first (you take the initiative to admit your mistake and lose 1%), which is safe. ✅
- **Excessive leverage (such as 20 times)**: Liquidation price is about 5% reverse - **almost coincident with your 5% Stop Loss** (including maintenance margin, handling fees, and funding fees, Liquidation may be **earlier**). This means that your Stop Loss has been Liquidated before it is triggered, or just after it was triggered - the 1% risk method is invalid. ❌

> ⚠️ Conclusion: **The leverage cannot be too high, and it must be ensured that the "Liquidation price" is significantly farther than the "Stop Loss price", leaving room for the Stop Loss to trigger. ** The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is, and the more likely it is to "Liquidate before Stop Loss", and your carefully designed 1% risk will be in vain.

In this way, the 1% risk method and liquidation prevention are connected into one logic: **Use low enough leverage → Keep the Liquidation price away from the Stop Loss price → Ensure that Stop Loss can be triggered before Liquidation → The risk of a single Stroke is truly locked at 1%. **

## 5. Complete logical chain of contract position management

String this article into an executable chain:

1. **Order Stroke Risk**: The maximum loss for each Stroke is 1% (or 2%) of the total funds.
2. **Calculate the nominal exposure based on the Stop Loss range**: Exposure = Risk amount ÷ Stop Loss range.
3. **Choose a low enough leverage**: Make sure that the Liquidation price is significantly further than the Stop Loss price (the higher the leverage, the closer it is to Liquidation, it is better to be low than high).
4. **Calculate the margin occupation based on exposure and leverage**.
5. **Strictly implement Stop Loss** (set before Liquidation price) and never passively wait for Liquidation.

What runs through this chain is the unchanging core of F4’s sentence: **"Survive" takes precedence over "earn more." ** Control the risk of a single Stroke, stay away from the Liquidation line, and allow yourself to survive in the market long enough - this is more important than the profit of any one Stroke.

# How Liquidation Happens Step by Step

> Midas Academy · C2 Contract Case Library (Virtual Suggestion Review)

Everyone has heard the word "Liquidation", but many people only know it as "losing money". This article uses a schematic case to show you the entire process of a Liquidation from opening a position to liquidation step by step - you will understand that it does not happen suddenly, but is approaching step by step, and why the actual Liquidation is often earlier than the theoretical price you calculated.

> ⚠️ The whole process is virtual/suggestive, and the numbers are all assumptions. It does not name the real targets, does not predict the real market, and does not constitute advice; this article aims to explain the risks clearly and does not encourage high leverage.

## 1. Scene setting

Trader F used **high leverage** Go Long (numerical fiction):

- **Margin 1,000 yuan, leverage 20 times, nominal exposure = 1,000 × 20 = 20,000 yuan**;
- **Opening price 100**, direction Go Long;
- First calculate the two key price points (E group caliber):
  - **Theoretical Liquidation price ≈ 100 × (1 − 1/20) = 95** (that is, if the reverse falls by 5%, the entire margin will be eaten up due to floating losses);
  - But because of the existence of **maintenance margin rate**, **actual liquidation will be triggered earlier than 95** (the price will be liquidated before it falls to 95), we assume that the actual liquidation price ≈ **95.5**.

20 times leverage means that as long as the reverse fluctuation is about 5%, the margin will return to zero - the fault tolerance space is as thin as paper.

## 2. Operation process (approaching Liquidation step by step)

The price did not rise but fell after opening the position. Let's see how F's margin was eaten up one by one (exposure 20,000, every 1% price drop = floating loss 200):

- **Price 100 → 98 (down 2%)**: Floating loss = 20000 × 2% = **400 yuan**, margin remaining 1000 − 400 = **600 yuan**;
- **Price 98 → 96 (down 4% in total)**: Floating loss = **800 yuan**, margin remaining **200 yuan** - already in danger;
- **Price 96 → 95.5 (a total decrease of 4.5%)**: Floating loss ≈ **900 yuan**, the margin remaining is about **100 yuan**, and has **touched the maintenance margin line** → **System Forced Liquidation (Liquidation)**, this Stroke's margin is basically lost.

Note: The price has not yet fallen to the theoretical Liquidation price of 95** (down 5%), but was liquidated at **95.5 (down 4.5%) - this is "actuality is earlier than theory".

## 3. Dismantling of key nodes

**Node 1: Why does the leverage of 20 times and the reverse of 5% explode? **
Because Liquidation price ≈ opening price ×(1 − 1/N), the larger N is, the closer it is to the opening price. A reverse fluctuation of 20 times → about (100÷20)% = **5%** will eat up the margin. **The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the position price and the smaller the error tolerance** (This is the core of Group E, also mentioned in C2-1). A 5% fluctuation is just "everyday" in many varieties, but it is enough to bring 20 times the position to zero.

**Node 2: Maintenance Margin Rate – Why Reality Comes Earlier Than Theory. **
The exchange requires that your margin cannot be lower than a **maintenance margin** level (a very small percentage). When your remaining margin drops to this maintenance line, the system will force liquidate -- instead of waiting for you to "lose nothing". Therefore, the actual liquidation price (95.5) is triggered earlier than the theoretical Liquidation price (95) of "margin return to zero". Many people calculated based on the theoretical price that they were "a little short of exploding". As a result, they were liquidated in advance. This is where they fell.

**Node 3: Liquidation is "approaching", not "sudden". **
From 100 to 98 to 96 to 95.5, F's margin went from 1000 → 600 → 200 → liquidation, which was **eaten step by step**. Every step along the way is actually an opportunity for **Stop Loss to leave the market** - if F falls below a certain preset level (such as 98, or earlier), it will Stop Loss and there will be no Liquidation at all. **Liquidation is not a natural disaster, but the inevitable end of "no Stop Loss along the way". **

**Node 4: Stop Loss could have avoided all of this. **
If F sets the Stop Loss before the Liquidation price (for example, if he leaves the market near 98), he will lose up to 400 and save most of his deposit. **Liquidation = Give up the opportunity of Stop Loss all the way until the system liquidates it for you. ** (Echoes C2-2)

## 4. Key points of review

- **Liquidation = The margin is completely eaten up (nearly eaten up), and the liquidation is forced by the system**; it is **approaching step by step**, not suddenly;
- **High leverage = minimal fault tolerance**: 20 times leverage will return to zero if the reverse direction is about 5% (Liquidation price ≈ opening × (1−1/N), long orders are below, and the larger N, the closer);
- **Actual Liquidation is often earlier than theory**: Because of the **Maintenance Margin Rate** - when the remaining margin touches the maintenance line, it will be liquidated. Don't press "return to zero" to calculate "it's still safe";
- **Every step is an opportunity for Stop Loss**: If you set the Stop Loss before the Liquidation price, you can take the initiative to leave the market halfway and avoid forced liquidation;
- **The root cause of Liquidation is excessive leverage + no Stop Loss**: Lowering leverage and setting up Stop Loss are the two most practical ways to stay away from Liquidation.

- Illustrative case: 20 times leverage, margin 1000, exposure 20000, opening position 100; **Theoretical Liquidation 95 (reverse 5%), maintaining margin leads to actual liquidation ≈ 95.5 (earlier)**; price 100→98 (remaining 600)→96 (remaining 200)→95.5 liquidation and loss.

- Liquidation is **approaching step by step**, and every step in the middle is a **Stop Loss opportunity**; the root cause is **too high leverage + no Stop Loss**.

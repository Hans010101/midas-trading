# Segments: A Higher-Level Unit of Trend

> Midas Academy · Level 3 · Module 10 Basic Concepts of Chan Theory

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Segment** consists of **at least three consecutive Strokes**, and **Strokes must overlap** (there is a price overlap area between the first Stroke and the third Stroke in the upward segment).
- Segment is one level higher than Stroke and is a smoother and more stable trend unit (Stroke constitutes Segment, reflecting level recursion).
- When the Segment** is effectively destroyed, a reverse Segment begins** (Segment end point = reverse Segment starting point).
- The precise rules for segmentation (Feature Sequence, etc.) are **advanced content**. This article only talks about the core concepts, and the details will be expanded according to standard definitions.

## 1. Concept introduction: Stroke is too thin and needs a more stable unit

In the last article we learned to draw Stroke. But there's a problem with Stroke: **it's more fragile**. A slight fluctuation in the market may cause a stroke. Looking at the stroke alone, the trend appears fragmented and unstable.

So Chan Theory defined a higher-level and more stable trend unit - **Segment** on top of Stroke. If Stroke is a "small step", Segment is a "staircase" that combines several small steps.

## 2. Definition of Segment: at least three Strokes, and there must be overlap

The definition of **Segment** is: ** consists of at least three consecutive Strokes, and there must be overlap between these Strokes. **

Take it apart and look at two key points:

1. **At least three Strokes**: A segment must be composed of at least three consecutive strokes (the direction of the strokes alternates up and down, so the upward segment starts with "up-down-up", and the downward segment starts with "down-up-down").
2. **There must be overlap**: There must be an **overlapping area in price** between the Strokes that make up the Segment - specifically, in the Upward Segment, the first Stroke (upward) and the third Stroke (upward) must have overlapping price ranges. If the second Stroke (retracement) falls too hard and the first Stroke and the third Stroke do not overlap at all, it will not constitute an effective upward segment.

The requirement of "overlap" is critical: it ensures that the Segment is a coherent and stable trend, rather than just a few Strokes forced together.

## 3. Level relationship between Segment and Stroke

Segment is one level higher than Stroke:

- **Stroke**: Connects the adjacent top Bottom Fractal, which is a smaller and finer unit.
- **Segment**: It is composed of at least three Strokes and is a higher-level, smoother and more stable unit.

They are a relationship of "the smallest constitutes the largest" - several Strokes combine to form a Segment, just as several K-lines combine to form Fractal and Stroke. This also echoes Chan Theory's idea of ​​"level recursion": the same "building block" relationship is repeated at different levels.

## 4. Division of Segment: If it is destroyed, the reverse Segment will start.

Segment will not continue indefinitely. When a Segment is effectively destroyed, it means that a reverse Segment begins - the original upward Segment ends and turns into a downward Segment (and vice versa). The end point of the Segment is the starting point of the reverse Segment.

So how do you tell if a Segment is "destroyed"? Chan Theory uses a set of methods called **"Feature Sequence"** to strictly judge (simply put, it is to observe whether the sequence composed of Strokes in the opposite direction to the Segment has a turning structure similar to Fractal).

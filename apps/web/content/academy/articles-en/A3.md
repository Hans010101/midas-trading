# What Candlestick Wicks Tell You

> **Midas Academy · Phase 1 · Module 2 Candlestick Basics**

**Quick Overview**

- **Shadows record the extreme positions prices reached but failed to defend**, leaving traces of the battle between bulls and bears.
- **Upper Shadow**: Price surged but was pushed back, reflecting selling pressure overhead.
- **Lower Shadow**: Price probed lower but was pulled back, reflecting buying support (floor) below.
- **Longer shadows indicate more intense battle between bulls and bears**; short shadows imply a decisive direction.
- Shadows are **signals of the battle, not guarantees of price movement**; long lower shadows (like the Hammer) are often seen as potential stabilization signals at low levels, but require subsequent verification and cannot be concluded based on a single candle.

## I. Concept Introduction: Shadows are the Traces of a "Fight"

In the previous lesson, we learned about the "Body" and "Shadow" of a candlestick. The Body tells the result from open to close, while **shadows record the "extreme positions" prices reached during this period but failed to defend**.

Think of it this way: The Body is the "final score," and the Shadow is the "struggle during the process." A candle with a long shadow often means bulls and bears fought intensely during this period—someone wanted to push up, someone wanted to push down, and finally, the price was pulled back, leaving a long "tail."

Understanding shadows allows you to see the battle beneath the surface of the price.

## II. Upper Shadow: There Are Sellers Above

**Upper Shadow** is the thin line above the body, with the top being the highest price of this period.

Its meaning is: **Price surged at one point but failed to stabilize and was pushed back.**

Why does this happen? When price surges upward, it encounters selling pressure from sellers—when there are more sellers, the price is driven back down, leaving a gap from the highest point at closing, resulting in an upper shadow.

Therefore, **a longer upper shadow typically reflects selling pressure overhead**: the price tested that height but was temporarily rejected by the market.

## III. Lower Shadow: There Are Buyers Below

**Lower Shadow** is the thin line below the body, with the bottom being the lowest price of this period.

Its meaning is exactly the opposite: **Price probed lower at one point but failed to continue falling and was pulled back.**

This indicates that when price fell to a certain level, it encountered buying support—when there are more buyers, the price is lifted back up, leaving a gap from the lowest point at closing, resulting in a lower shadow.

Therefore, **a longer lower shadow typically reflects support (buying support) below**: the price tested that low level but was blocked by buyers.

## IV. Shadow Length: How Intense Is the Battle?

Shadows are not just about existence; they have length, and **length reflects the intensity of the battle between bulls and bears**:

- **Very short or almost no shadows**: Indicates the price direction during this period was relatively "decisive," with little retracement. For example, a large bullish candle with almost no upper or lower shadows means it was basically bought up steadily from open to close.
- **Long shadows**: Indicates significant retracement and intense fighting. For example, a long upper shadow is "surged up and pushed down"; a long lower shadow is "smashed down and pulled up."

It is important to emphasize one point: **Shadows are "signals of the battle," not "guarantees of rising or falling."** A long lower shadow tells you "buying support existed here," but it does not equal "it will definitely rise next." It only provides a clue about supply and demand; true judgment requires a comprehensive view of position, trend, and other information. Treating a single shadow as an "ironclad rule" of price movement is the most common mistake for beginners.

## V. A Typical Example: Long Lower Shadow (The Hammer)

Amplifying the characteristic of a "long lower shadow" gives rise to a pattern repeatedly discussed in practice—the **Hammer**.

The Hammer looks like this: **A very small body located in the upper part of the entire candle, with a long lower shadow dragging below (usually the lower shadow length is significantly longer than the body), and a very short or no upper shadow**, looking like a hammer overall.

Its meaning is: During this period, price was heavily beaten down but subsequently pulled back strongly by buyers, closing back above—showcasing obvious support from below.

In practice, **if a Hammer appears in a low area after a downtrend, it is often regarded as a "potential stabilization/reversal signal" worth noting**. But remember the sentence above: It is a "signal," not a "promise." A single Hammer itself cannot confirm anything. The professional approach is to **wait for subsequent candles and other information for further verification**, rather than jumping to conclusions upon seeing a Hammer.

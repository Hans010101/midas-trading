# Common Mistakes and Traps in Contract Trading

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types (Contract Practice)

**Quick overview of this article**

- Five common mistakes in contract practice: **full position/high leverage, taking orders without Stop Loss, ignoring capital costs, frequent opening/excessive trading, and treating the contract as a casino**.
- Their common core: **It may be a minor problem in the spot, but it is magnified into a fatal injury by leverage in the contract** - What the contract amplifies is the "wrong price".
- Corresponding disciplines: low leverage, strict Stop Loss (set before Liquidation price), position control (1% risk method), restraint from excessive trading, and do not treat contracts as gambling.
- Ways to avoid pitfalls: **Make mistakes first at zero cost in a virtual environment + Discipline enforcement + Respect risks**.
- ⚠️ Red line: The contract amplifies the cost of mistakes; emphasizes virtual practice and disciplined execution; **Does not guarantee profit, does not predict, does not constitute investment advice**, and what was effective in the past does not mean it will be effective in the future.

## 1. Concept introduction: The contract will amplify every mistake you make

This concludes the practical contract series. In the last article, we will make a "**Contra List**" - a centralized list of the most common mistakes and traps that novices make in contracts.

Why talk specifically about "errors"? Because the contract has one of the most distinctive features:

**It will use leverage to multiply the cost of every mistake you make. **

The same is "no Stop Loss". In the spot price, it may just be a floating loss for a while, but in the contract, it may be **Liquidation is out**. Therefore, in a contract, "making fewer mistakes" is more important than "making more money". This article is to help you identify these pitfalls and avoid them in advance.

## 2. Mistake 1: Full position, high leverage

This is the most common and fatal mistake, bar none.

- "A shuttle", dozens or even hundreds of times leverage - many people think this is a "shortcut to amplify profits".
- The truth is (review E4, F23): **The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the position price, and the smaller the room for error**. With 50 times leverage, a reverse movement of about 2% is close to Liquidation - and a 2% fluctuation may be a matter of minutes in the crypto market.
- **High leverage + full position, the essence is not to "amplify profits", but to "amplify risks and accelerate exit". ** If you make a right bet, you earn one Stroke, but if you make a wrong bet, it may all go back to zero - this is not a transaction, it is a gamble on life.

> ⚠️ Reiterating the red line: The other side of amplifying gains is always **equally amplifying losses, possibly Liquidation**.

## 3. Mistake 2: Carrying the order and not Stop Loss (then F5, F6)

The second fatal mistake is not to Stop Loss if you lose money, but to hold on to the money in the hope that it will "come back".

- This is the "**Hope**" emotional trap that F5 talks about - wishful thinking and waiting for the return of capital when there is a sudden loss, instead of admitting the mistake as planned.
- This is also what F6 talks about: **Loss of Discipline** - Stop Loss is clearly set, but canceled or moved back on the spot.
- In contracts, carrying orders is particularly fatal: Leverage amplifies floating losses. The longer you carry the trade, the more you lose. Liquidation is often the result of carrying the trade in the end. Not Stop Loss is equivalent to leaving life and death to the market.

The contract does not give you the opportunity to "hold it for a long time and wait for it to come back" - this is completely different from spot (review E8, F19). **In the contract, Stop Loss is not a multiple choice question, it is a necessity for survival. **

## 4. Mistake 3: Ignoring the capital cost (continue E2, F6)

Many people focus on the rise and fall of prices, but completely ignore the ongoing cost of capital fees.

- If you hold a Perpetual Contract that requires payment, the capital fee will **continue to erode** your income (review E2); when held for a long time, the accumulation of Stroke fees cannot be ignored.
- If you trade frequently, **handling fees** will also be superimposed - the more transactions you make, the more costs will be incurred (review the dangers of excessive trading in F6).
- Result: Some people got the price direction right, but ** after including capital fees and handling fees, they made no profit or even lost **.

The profit and loss of a contract never only depends on the price - "invisible costs" such as capital fees and handling fees must be included.

## 5. Mistake 4: Frequent opening and closing, excessive trading (then F6)

The contract market is open 24/7, coupled with the strong stimulation brought by leverage, it is particularly easy for people to fall into the addictive habit of opening and closing positions frequently.

- **Cost Erosion**: There is a handling fee for every opening, and the cost of frequent transactions is staggering;
- **Emotion and Fatigue**: People who are addicted to watching the market and constantly operating will become tired, their judgment will be distorted, and they will be more likely to make mistakes (Review F6);
- **Deviation from the system**: Good opportunities that truly comply with the rules are limited. Frequent trading often means that you are making a large number of impulse orders "outside the rules".

Remember the words of F6: **When there is no opportunity to comply with the rules, "not trading" is also a correct operation. ** In the 24-hour contract market, it is especially important to overcome "itchy hands".

## 6. Mistake 5: Treating the contract as a casino (continue E8)

This is the most fundamental **mentality error**: no analysis, no Stop Loss, and relying on feelings to "get a chance" with a large position - treating the contract as a gamble rather than a decision-making based on method and probability.

- The **rapid feedback** of the contract (profits and losses come quickly and violently) are most likely to stimulate **gambling** (review E8's emotional risk);
- Once you treat trading as gambling, what you compete with is not judgment and discipline, but luck -** and if you rely on luck for a long time, the outcome is almost certain to**.

The difference between trading and gambling lies in whether there is a system based on probability and risk management (recall F1). Treating a contract as a casino is the most dangerous mistake of all.

## 7. What these mistakes have in common: the cost of being magnified by leverage

Putting the above five errors together, they have a common core:

**They may be just "minor problems" in the spot market, but in the contract, they are magnified into a "fatal injury" by leverage. **

- If the spot position is full, it will be locked up at best; if the contract position is full + high leverage, liquidation may occur instantly.
- If you place an order in the spot, you can still wait; if you place an order in the contract, you can often wait for Liquidation.
- Frequent transactions in spot transactions will lead to handling fees; frequent transactions in contracts will also add capital fees and leverage risks.

** What contracts amplify is never just the benefits, but also the "price of mistakes." ** This is why contracts are so unfriendly to newbies - the penalties for mistakes are much harsher than spot.

## 8. How to avoid: virtual practice + discipline enforcement

There are no shortcuts to avoid these pitfalls, but there are clear principles:

1. **Practice in the virtual environment first** (Review F10, F18): Make these mistakes in the virtual environment at zero cost** - In the full virtual environment of Midas, experience a full liquidation and a liquidation of an order, which is more profound than any preaching.
2. **Low leverage, strict Stop Loss, and position control** (Review F23): Use low enough leverage, set Stop Loss before the Liquidation price, and use the 1% risk method to control the single Stroke.
3. **Discipline Enforcement** (Review F6): Follow the rules set in advance, and don’t be carried away by emotions and flukes on the spot.
4. **Awe of risk** (Review E8): Always remember that contracts are high-risk instruments, and do not be carried away by the temptation of "amplifying returns".

# Moving Average (MA): The Most Basic Trend Indicator

> **Midas Academy · Phase 2 · Module 6 Classic Technical Indicators**

**Quick Overview**

- **Moving Average (MA)** = The average of the closing prices of the last N periods, calculated rolling and connected into a line; it is smoother than price, making it easier to see the trend.
- MA can be **approximated as the "average holding cost" during this period** (for understanding purposes only).
- **The direction of the MA reflects the trend**: Upward for Long, Downward for Short, Flat for consolidation; however, it reflects the *past* trend, not a prediction of the future.
- **Short-term MAs are sensitive, long-term MAs are stable**; there is no good or bad period—choose based on your observation goal.

## I. Concept Introduction: Smoothing Volatility to See Direction

If you look at candlesticks one by one, the price goes up and down, making it hard to see the direction at a glance. Is there a way to "smooth out" these chaotic fluctuations so the trend becomes clearer? **Moving Average (MA)** is exactly for that—it is the most basic and commonly used technical indicator and the foundation for learning Golden Crosses, Death Crosses, Bollinger Bands, and other content.

## II. How MA is Calculated

The full name of MA is "Moving Average." Breaking it down reveals its algorithm:

**Take the closing prices of the last N periods, calculate the average to get a point; as time passes, continuously recalculate rolling, and connect these points into a line.**

For example, the algorithm for "5-Day MA" (denoted as MA5) is: **The average of the closing prices of the last 5 days.**

- Today's MA5 = (Today + the previous 4 days, total 5 days' closing prices) ÷ 5.
- Tomorrow, you recalculate using "the 5 days counting back from tomorrow"—dropping the oldest day and adding the newest day, causing the average to "move."

Connecting the calculated averages for each day results in that smooth MA line. (Here we refer to the most common "Simple Moving Average," where each day's closing price has equal weight; there are also variants like "Exponential Moving Average (EMA)" which give higher weight to recent prices, used later in MACD.)

Because MA uses an "average over a period," extreme daily fluctuations are averaged out, so **MA is always smoother than the price itself**, and its direction is easier to discern. When overlaid on candlesticks, the sense of trend becomes immediately clear.

## III. What MA Represents: The Approximate "Average Holding Cost"

MA is not just a mathematical curve; it has an intuitive meaning: **It can be approximated as the "average holding cost" of market participants during this period.**

The logic is simple: The N-Day MA is the average of the closing prices over these N days, and buyers during this period generally have costs distributed around these prices. Therefore, MA20 roughly reflects the "average cost level of buyers over the last 20 periods."

This understanding is very useful:

- When the price is **above the MA**, it means the current price is higher than the average cost of this period, and **holders are on average in floating profit**.
- When the price is **below the MA**, it means the current price is lower than the average cost, and **holders are on average in floating loss**.

(Note: This is an **approximate, helpful perspective**. Strictly speaking, MA only uses closing prices and does not account for volume or intraday prices, so it is not equal to the precise real cost.)

## IV. The Direction of MA Reflects the Trend

Since MA approximates the average cost, its **direction** is very significant:

- **MA is Upward**: The average holding cost is constantly rising, indicating buyers are willing to take over at increasingly higher prices—usually corresponding to a **Long (uptrend) structure**.
- **MA is Downward**: The average cost is constantly falling, usually corresponding to a **Short (downtrend) structure**.
- **MA is Flat**: The average cost is not changing much, often corresponding to **consolidation**.

Therefore, just looking at the tilt of a single MA gives you a rough judgment of the current trend. But it must be emphasized: **MA reflects the "past" trend, not a prediction of the future**—this will be more obvious in the next article on Golden and Death Crosses.

## V. MAs of Different Periods: Sensitivity vs. Stability

The "N" in MA can take different values, resulting in MAs of different periods with distinct characteristics:

- **Short-term MAs** (e.g., MA5, MA10): Because they average only a few days, they react **sensitively to recent price changes and turn quickly**, but are also more susceptible to short-term fluctuations and "fake moves."
- **Long-term MAs** (e.g., MA60, MA120): Averaging over a long time, they are **smooth, stable, and reflect the big direction**, but they are **lagging** and turn slowly.

Think of it this way: Short-term MAs are like a "little follower" running tight to the price, while long-term MAs are like the "big direction" moving unhurriedly. No period is better; **short ones are sensitive, long ones are stable—choose based on what you want to observe.** Placing different period MAs together is the foundation for the next "Golden and Death Crosses" and "Multi-MA System."

# Funding Rate Arbitrage: Principles, Direction, and Why 'Arbitrage' ≠ Risk-Free

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types (Contract Practice)

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Funding Rate arbitrage = holding spot + contract positions with the opposite direction and equivalent size, hedging the directional risk, and only charging funding fees** (delta neutral idea).
- **Direction** (consistent with E2): If you are long with a positive rate, you will pay a short position → you will be charged for holding a short position in the contract, and you will be hedged with a long spot position; if you are short with a negative rate, you will be charged a long position → you will be charged if you are holding a long position in the contract (the actual operation is more complicated).
- ⚠️ **"Arbitrage" is by no means risk-free**: There are five major risks: basis fluctuation, Liquidation (the most fatal if the contract leg is liquidated, the hedging is broken), rate reversal, transaction costs, and liquidity; it replaces directional risk with a basket of other risks.
- It is a **conceptual science popularization, not a tutorial on making sure money**; the benefits are meager but the risks are real. Beginners should not be misled by "arbitrage is risk-free".
- ⚠️ Red line: Arbitrage is not risk-free, the contract legs will be liquidated, the rates will be reversed, and the costs will eat up the profits; **Profits are not guaranteed, do not constitute operational recommendations, and are not predicted**, and what was valid in the past does not mean it will be valid in the future.

## 1. Introduction of the concept: "Eat the money and earn money", is it true?

In the crypto community, you have most likely heard of sayings such as "Funding Rate arbitrage", "Neutral arbitrage eats the funding fees", and "Lying and earning the funding fees". It sounds beautiful: you don’t need to judge the rise or fall, just rely on collecting capital fees and make money steadily.

Is it really that good?

In this article, on the one hand, we will explain clearly the **principle and direction** of Funding Rate arbitrage, and on the other hand - and most importantly - we will expose its biggest misunderstanding: **"arbitrage" does not mean "risk-free". ** Put the most important words here in advance:

> ⚠️ Funding Rate arbitrage is a concept of "neutral strategy", but it is not "risk-free profit". It just replaces "directional risk" with a series of other risks such as basis fluctuations, Liquidation, rate reversal, transaction costs, liquidity, etc. This article is a popular science concept, not a tutorial on making money, and does not constitute any operational advice. **

## 2. First review: Funding Rate (quote E2, do not expand repeatedly)

We talked about the mechanism of Funding Rate in E2. Here we only review the conclusion (no further details):

- The long and short parties of the Perpetual Contract receive and pay each other a Stroke fee at regular intervals, which is used to anchor the perpetual price to the spot;
- **Positive fee = longs pay to shorts; negative fee = shorts pay to longs**;
- This Stroke money is a transfer between long and short positions, not a handling fee paid to the platform.

Funding Rate arbitrage is to find a way to stand on the side of "charges" and at the same time hedge the risk of price increases and decreases, thereby "netly collecting" the Stroke fee.

## 3. Basic idea of Funding Rate arbitrage: spot + contract hedging

The core idea can be summarized in one sentence: **Hold "spot" and "contract" positions of equal size in opposite directions at the same time, so that the price increases and decreases on both sides offset each other (directional risks are hedged), thereby only earning/collecting funding fees. **

Take the most common **positive rate** situation as an example:

- When the rate is positive, **the long contract has to pay to the short**. Well, if you hold a **short** contract, you are on the **charge** side.
- But there is directional risk in only holding short contracts (you will lose money if the price goes up). So, you simultaneously buy an equal amount of spot long positions to hedge:
  - Price **increase**: The floating profit of the long spot position is offset by the floating loss of the short contract position;
  - Price **Fall**: The floating loss of the spot long position is offset by the floating profit of the short contract position.
- The directional profits and losses on both sides **offset each other**. Ideally, you don't bet on the rise or fall, and **netly charge** the capital fee for the short position under the positive rate.

This is the so-called "neutral" (delta neutral) idea: Don't bet on the direction, only the rate. **

## 4. The direction must be correct

Align the arbitrage direction with the mechanism of E2, and be sure to remember it correctly (if the direction is wrong, the entire logic will be reversed):

- **Positive rate**: longs pay shorts → If you want to charge, just **hold short contracts** (the charging party), and use **spot longs** to hedge directional risks.
- **Negative fee rate**: short to pay for long → If you want to charge, just **hold long contracts** (the charging party) and use spot/reverse positions to hedge (the actual operation is more complicated).

In a word: ** arbitrage is "standing on the side of charging, while using spot money to hedge the direction risk." **

## 5. ★Why "arbitrage" does not mean risk-free (key points)

This is the core of the whole article. The so-called "risk-free arbitrage" is a dangerous illusion - the above picture of "perfect hedging and net charge rate" is full of risk points in reality:

**1. Basis fluctuation risk. **
Spot and contract prices do not always match up exactly (the difference between the two is the **basis**, review E7). The basis will **fluctuate** - the hedging of the two legs is not perfect, and changes in the basis will bring additional profit and loss fluctuations, **even cause losses** when it is unfavorable. You think the direction is locked, but in fact you are still exposed to changes in basis.

**2. Liquidation risk (the most deadly). **
That leg of the contract is the one with leverage. If margin is not managed properly and prices fluctuate wildly, **the contract leg may liquidate** (review E4).

> ⚠️ Once the contract leg is liquidated, your hedging structure will be **instantly broken** - leaving only the unhedged spot leg (or vice versa), all directional risks will be exposed, which may result in **huge losses**. This is the most fatal risk of Funding Rate arbitrage: I originally wanted to be "neutral", but due to the liquidation of the contract leg, it became a unilateral naked exposure.

**3. Rate reverse risk. **
Funding Rate will change. When you open a position, the rate is positive (you are charged), but the rate may turn negative (you have to pay). Once the rate reverses, your arbitrage gains will not only disappear, but may also be reversed. Rates are not locked, they change at any time.

**4. Transaction costs. **
Both opening and closing positions require **handling fees** and **slippage**. The Funding Rate itself is often very meager, and these costs may easily eat up or even exceed the rate income you earn. "It seems profitable", but not necessarily after deducting costs.

**5. Liquidity risk. **
Under extreme market conditions, the market may fluctuate violently and deteriorate in depth, and you may not be able to close your position or adjust your two legs in time, resulting in hedging imbalance and passive exposure to risks.

**Put these five points together and it becomes clear: the "profit" of Funding Rate arbitrage is meager and the certainty is questionable, while its "risks" are real and diverse. ** It does not eliminate risks, it just replaces directional risks into a basket of risks of basis, Liquidation, rates, costs, and liquidity. Any propaganda that says it is a "risk-free way to make money" is not credible.

## 6. Positioning of this article: conceptual science popularization, not a tutorial on making sure money

The positioning of this article must be clear:

- It is a science popularization of the **concept** of "neutral strategy/Funding Rate arbitrage", helping you understand its principles, directions and risks;
- It is **not** a "just follow this and you'll make money" tutorial - real execution involves complicated leg ratios, margin management, dynamic rebalancing and risk control, which is far more complicated than the "principles";
- **Newbies, especially, should not be misled by "arbitrage without risk"** and start hastily - once the contract legs are liquidated, "arbitrage" will instantly turn into a huge loss.

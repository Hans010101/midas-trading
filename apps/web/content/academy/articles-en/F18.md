## 1. Concept introduction: How do you know whether a strategy will work?

After learning so many strategies, an unavoidable question arises: **Will this strategic idea in my hand work? **

The worst answer is: "Just take real money and try it." - That is equivalent to using the principal to pay Stroke's tuition, which may be expensive.

The correct approach is: test first and then practice. ** This article, as the end of this module (F11–F18), talks about how to turn a strategic idea into a quantifiable test rule, use Midas' **Strategy Laboratory** backtest to see its historical performance, understand the backtest indicators, and then use **virtual trading** to verify the execution - complete the closed loop of "learning → using". This is also the focus of the entire training camp's "unity of knowledge and action".

## 2. Step one: Turn "ideas" into "quantifiable rules"

A vague idea cannot be tested. For example, "buy when there is a breakthrough" - what is a breakthrough? Breakthrough of what? How much to buy? When will it be sold? If these are not clear, there is no way to objectively verify them.

The first step in testing is to translate ideas** into clear, quantifiable, and unambiguous rules**, which at least include:

- **Entry Conditions**: Specific signals/prices to enter the market (such as "the price closes above the 20-day high").
- **Exit conditions**: Where is the Stop Loss and where is the Take Profit (or what mobile Stop Loss rules are used).
- **Position Rules**: How much to invest and how much risk to take for each Stroke (response to F4).

**The clearer and more objective the rules are, the more they can be tested; vague "feelings" cannot be back-tested. ** This step itself will force you to think clearly about your strategy - many ideas that "sound good" are revealed to be unclear and contradictory when they are forced to be written into precise rules.

## 3. Step 2: Use "Strategy Research Laboratory" to backtest

After the rules are clear, you can **backtest**.

**Backtesting uses historical market data to simulate how this set of rules will perform in the past. **Midas' **Strategy Lab** provides a backtest function: run your rules on historical data and get a **Strategy Net Value Curve** and a series of **Performance Indicators**, allowing you to intuitively see "what would have been the result if you had done this in the past."

(Again: This is in a **full virtual** environment, used for analysis and testing, and does not involve any real funds.)

## 4. Step 3: Understand the backtest indicators

Backtesting will give you a bunch of indicators. The most common mistake for novices is to only focus on "how much they earn"**. In fact, we need to look at several key indicators comprehensively:

- **Cumulative Return**: The total return during the entire backtest period. **Don’t just look at this** – high returns may come with huge risks that you can’t bear.
- **Maximum Drawdown**: The **Maximum Drawdown** from the highest point of the fund to the lowest point thereafter. It measures "how much your account has lost during the most difficult time" and is a key indicator of risk. A strategy with a high cumulative return but a large maximum drawdown (for example, a drawdown of more than half halfway) is likely to cause you to give up in the middle of the real offer because you can't bear it. For a strategy with a drawdown that is too large, no matter how high the return is, it may not be relevant to you.
- **Win Rate**: Proportion of profitable trades. Recall F3: **High winning rate ≠ Make money**, it should be looked at together with the profit and loss ratio.
- **Profit and loss ratio**: average profit ÷ average loss. Recall F3: It must be looked at in conjunction with the winning rate to determine whether the "money-making logic" of the strategy is established.

**Looking at it comprehensively, we should pay special attention to "maximum drawdown" and "profit and loss ratio", rather than just looking at cumulative returns. ** A strategy that can be executed in the long term is often not the one with the highest return, but the one with a balance between return and drawdown that allows you to sleep well.

## 5. ★The fourth step (the most important warning): valid for backtest ≠ valid for future

This section is the most important red line of the entire article and the function of this product. Please remember:

**Just because a strategy performs well in backtesting does not necessarily mean that it will make money in the future. ** There are three reasons:

**1. Overfitting (curve fitting). **
If you repeatedly adjust the parameters until the backtest results are "very beautiful" - it is likely that you just overfit the rules to this specific historical data, and remember the historical "noise" rather than the rules. This kind of "perfect backtest" often fails immediately if it is switched to the future and unseen market conditions. **The more beautiful the backtest is, the more wary it is that it is a product of overfitting. **

**2. Survivor bias. **
If you only backtest on the varieties/data that are "still alive" and ignore those that have been delisted, disappeared, or returned to zero, the result will be a systematic overestimation of the performance of the strategy - because you only count the "survivors" and exclude the losers.

**3. History does not simply repeat itself. **
Markets change (which is the core of F17). What worked in the past does not mean it will work in the future. **Backtesting reflects the past, while what you want to trade is the future** - there is no inevitable bridge between the two.

**So, the correct positioning of backtesting is: a tool to "eliminate obviously unreliable rules and understand the characteristics of the strategy (such as how big its retracement is, and under what market conditions it makes money)", rather than a guarantee to "prove that it can make money in the future." ** Backtesting can help you eliminate bad strategies, but it cannot help you "verify" that a strategy will work in the future.

## 6. Step 5: Use virtual real disk to verify execution

After backtesting (looking at history), you need to use **virtual trading** to verify a period of time forward:

- In the **virtual trading** environment of Midas, according to your rules, you can actually run for a period of time in the **forward and close to real** market conditions.
- It tests two things: ① How the strategy performs in "**markets it has never seen**" (closer to reality than simple backtesting); ② **Whether you can strictly implement it** - many strategies fail in execution (F6), not in the rules themselves.

This step will test not only the strategy, but also your execution and mentality. A strategy that backtests beautifully, but you can't actually grasp and maintain discipline, is also ineffective for you.

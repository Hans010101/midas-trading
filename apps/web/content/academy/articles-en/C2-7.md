# Using Both Long and Short Positions

> Midas Academy · C2 Contract Case Library (Virtual Suggestion Review)

One of the major features of contracts compared with spot prices is that they can move in both directions - if it goes up, it goes long, and if it goes down, it goes short. This article uses a schematic case to see how the two-way operation of "Go Short first, then Go Long" works, and also talk about the idea of ​​"hedging". But let’s pour some cold water on it first: **Two-way is “more flexible” and by no means “guaranteed profit”. **

> ⚠️ The entire process is virtual/indicative, no real targets are named, no predictions of real market conditions are made, and no recommendations are constituted.

## 1. Scene setting

Set a period of "first down and then up" market trend** as indicated by ** (numerical fiction):

- A certain variety first fell from **110 to 90**, and then rose back to 110** (a V-shaped trend that first goes down and then goes up indicates the trend);
- Trader I wants to take advantage of this two-way fluctuation, **Go Short first, then Go Long**;
- Note: This is an indicative trend that is **very clear in hindsight**; in reality, you **don't know** that it will go like this - this will be emphasized later.

## 2. Operation process (empty first, then long)

Two-stage operation of I:

- **First paragraph·Go Short**: At **110**, judge that the short-term is weak, **Open Short**, and set Stop Loss (Go ShortStop Loss is above **); if the price is judged to go down, it will reach around **95** I **Close ShortTake Profit** - This period of Go Short makes a profit (110→95);
- **Second segment·Go Long**: The price fell to around 90 and stabilized, I judged it was going to rebound, **turned to Open Long** (for example, **95** entered the market), and also set Stop Loss (Go LongStop Loss is **below**); the price rose to around **108**, I **Close LongTake Profit** - this period Go Long made another profit (95→108).

Both periods were in the right direction, and I made **profit in two periods** during this two-way fluctuation. It looks beautiful – but read on for the key teardown.

## 3. Dismantling of key nodes

**Node 1: Two-way is "more flexible", not "guaranteed profit". **
I was right in both paragraphs this time. It was ** who signaled the market for him to be right with ** - the V-shape was very clear in hindsight. But in reality, you don’t know in advance that it will fall first and then rise: when Go Short, it may not fall but rise, and when Go Long, it may not rise but fall. **Both directions give you the flexibility to "participate in ups and downs", but you may be wrong in either direction**. Don't misunderstand "can go both ways" as "can make money on both sides".

**Node 2: Each direction must be independently judged + independent Stop Loss. **
Go Short and Go Long are two independent Stroke transactions, each requiring: independent reasons for entry, independent Stop Loss, and independent risk control. I When Go Short, the Stop Loss is set **above**, when Go Long, the Stop Loss is set **bottom** - the directions are different, and the Stop Loss direction is also opposite. **Don't blindly and confidently backhand the Go Long position just because "you made money on the Go Short in the previous period". **

![C2-7 with picture 2](/academy-img/C2-7-配图2.png)

**Node 3: The risks of Go Short are symmetrical (be even more careful). **
Many novices think that "Go Short is as simple as Go Long", but remember: **When Go Short, the rising price is the direction of loss, and the Liquidation price of the short position is [above] the opening price** (Group E caliber). Moreover, although Go Short can charge a fee in the **positive rate** market, the short risk is also very real during **short squeeze/surge**. **Go Short is not as easy as "go Long the other way around". Risks also exist and Stop Loss must also be set. **

**Node 4: By the way, let’s talk about the “hedging” idea (≠ eliminate risk). **
Two-way can also be used to **hedge**: For example, if you hold **spot longs** and are worried about short-term declines, you can **open a short contract** to hedge the risk of declines (short positions will make profits when they fall, and hedging spot losses). But be sure to be sober (echoing F22):

- Hedging** reduces directional risk, but does not eliminate all risks**;
- Hedging **has costs** (funding fees, opportunity costs - short losses will eat up the increase in spot prices when prices rise);
- The hedging leg (short contract) itself** also has leverage and Liquidation risk**.

**"Hedging" in no way equates to "risk-free". **

## 4. Key points of review

- **Two-way is "more flexible", not "guaranteed profit"**: It can go up Go Long and fall Go Short, but **you may be wrong in each direction**; don't think "can go both ways" as "both ends take it all";
- **Independent judgment for each direction + independent Stop Loss**: Go LongStop Loss is below, Go ShortStop Loss is above; **Go ShortLiquidation price is above the opening price** (E group caliber), the risk is symmetrical and cannot be underestimated;
- **Don’t blindly backhand a large position just because you made money in the previous period**: The profit and loss of the previous Stroke has nothing to do with the judgment of the next Stroke (the result of being right or wrong, F7);
- **Hedging ≠ Eliminate risk**: long spot + short contract can hedge against decline, but **Reducing direction risk ≠ eliminate risk, there is cost, and the hedging leg also has Liquidation risk** (echoing F22);
- **Indicates that the market situation will be clear after the fact, but the truth is unknown beforehand**: Don’t be fooled by the “V-shaped review” and think that it is easy to catch both sides.

One sentence to end: **The value of two-way trading is to allow you to "have something to do in more market conditions", rather than "allowing you to make money at both ends." ** Every direction must be treated as a completely new Stroke transaction that may be misunderstood.

# Review of a Failed Trade: What to Learn from Losses

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 19 Practical Case Review

**Quick overview of this article**

- Use a schematic case to demonstrate a failed Stroke transaction (**Go against the trend and catch the flying knife + No Stop Loss → Big loss/Liquidation**), which belongs to the four quadrants of F7 "Make a mistake + Lose money = Must change".
- Dismantling: **Wrong judgment (methodological level: should not go against the trend and buy the bottom → change the method)** + **Execution level (discipline level: should not Stop Loss → change the discipline)**, both are wrong in this case; only by distinguishing the two types of problems can we solve the problem.

## 1. Concept introduction: Loss is the best teacher

The previous article demonstrated a Stroke's smooth trend trading. But in real trading, **losses are the norm** - no matter how mature a trader is, they will lose money. Therefore, compared to "money-making cases", learning from "failure cases" often yields greater gains**.

In this article, we use a **schematic** failure case to demonstrate how to **learn real things** from a Stroke loss transaction - instead of falling into regret, self-blame, or simply forgetting about it.

First put the red line in front:

> ⚠️ This article uses **teaching and general illustrative cases** and **does not name any real stocks/varieties**. The purpose of reviewing failure cases is to calmly dismantle "what went wrong and how to correct it", and only teach lessons and never humiliate people (whether it is others or themselves). The market is a sign, entry and exit are logical signs, not trading signals.

## 2. Case setting (suggestion)

Set up a scenario where it is easy for novices to get into trouble (gesturing throughout, without naming names):

- There is a **schematic downtrend** (lower highs and lower lows - a typical downtrend);
- A trader made two **the most common fatal mistakes**: **buying against the trend and "catching the flying knife"** + **not taking the Stop Loss**;
- Let's restore this process and dismantle it using the methods we have learned.

## 3. The process of error occurrence (restore "at that time")

From the perspective of "that time", let's reconstruct how this Stroke transaction led to a big loss step by step:

**The first step: counter the trend and buy the bottom (catching the flying knife). **
Prices have been falling for some time and are in a clear **downtrend**. The trader thought to himself: "It has fallen so much, it should be almost the end, right?" - So, in the absence of any stabilization/reversal signal, they **buy against the downward trend and buy the bottom** (this is the most dangerous type of "left-side bottom buying" mentioned in F16 - **catching a flying knife**).

**Step 2: Don’t Stop Loss and carry the order. **
After buying, the price did not rebound as he expected, but continued to fall. The correct approach at this time is to stop loss and leave the market as planned. But he did not set Stop Loss (or set it but did not execute it), but held on to it with the thought that "it will always rise back" (this is the "Hope" emotional trap that F5 talks about).

**Step 3: Expansion of losses. **
Prices continue to fall, and floating losses are getting bigger and bigger. At the end of the day, you either have to cut the flesh and admit a big loss with a Stroke, or (if it is a contract and leverage is used) directly **Liquidation** out (Review E4: The floating loss is close to the margin and it is liquidated).

A stroke that could have been a "small loss exit" transaction turned into a "big loss / Liquidation" due to these two mistakes.

## 4. Use F7 to position the four quadrants: this is "making a mistake + losing money"

First, use the four quadrants of F7 to determine the position of this Stroke transaction: its ** process is wrong** (buying the bottom against the trend + not stopping loss violates the method and discipline), and the ** result is also a loss** - falling into the "**wrong + losing money**" category, which is the category that F7 said "**must change**".

Now that the positioning is clear, the next step is the core of the review: What went wrong? Is it a wrong judgment or wrong execution? **

## 5. Dismantling: Wrong judgment or wrong execution? (continue F7)

F7 said that review should distinguish between "errors in judgment (method issues)" and "errors in execution (discipline issues)" - because their **directions of improvement are completely different**. This Stroke failed transaction, **both types of errors were made**:

**Wrong judgment (methodological level): You should not go against the trend and buy the bottom. **
In a clear **downtrend**, buying the bottom without any stabilization signal is **violating the principle of "following the trend/waiting for confirmation"** (reviewing the extremely high risk of F8 following the trend and F16 buying the bottom on the left). This is a **methodological** fault - he chose a style of play (catching a throwing knife) with a very low probability of winning and a very high risk. **Improvement direction: Change the method** - Don't go against the trend in a downward trend, either follow the trend or wait for real stabilization/reversal confirmation (right side).

**Execution error (discipline level): Stop Loss and take orders should not be allowed. **
To take a step back, **even if the entry judgment is wrong**, if he **sets Stop Loss and strictly implements it**, the Stroke will only lose 1% at most (looking back at F4), and it will never turn into a big loss/Liquidation. But he didn't have a Stop Loss and still carried orders - this was his disciplinary fault. **Improvement direction: Change discipline** - Stop Loss must be set, must be executed, and never take orders.

![F27 with picture 2](/academy-img/F27-配图2.png)

**This Stroke transaction is a double mistake of "judgment + execution"**: In terms of method, you should not take the flying knife, and in terms of discipline, you should not take the order. The value of review lies in seeing these two types of problems separately and treating them accordingly - rather than simply saying "I'm too good".

## 6. How emotions work (then F5)

Going deeper, look at the role that emotion (F5) plays behind the scenes - because many "errors in judgment/execution" are rooted in emotions:

- **"It's time to rebound after falling so much"**: This is a kind of **bottom-buying fluke/greed**--wanting to buy at the lowest point and take advantage, thus ignoring the objective fact of the downward trend.
- **Carry orders without Stop Loss**: This is the **"hope"** emotion (F5) at work - wishful thinking of looking forward to getting back the capital when losing money, and unwilling to face "I was wrong".
- The whole process was caused by **emotion overriding the rules**: If it had been executed mechanically according to the pre-existing rules (Follow the Trend, Stop Loss), both of these mistakes could have been avoided.

This confirms the core of F5 and F6: fighting emotions relies on rules and discipline beforehand, rather than "I feel" during the matter. **

## 7. ★Key: Pay attention to the matter rather than the result, and only pay attention to the lesson after failure.

When reviewing failed cases, there are two most important attitudes (both connected to F7):

**1. Be right about the matter but not the result - even if you get lucky, it is still "dangerous". **
Assume another situation: This time he took the flying knife + carried the order, and got lucky, the price really rebounded, and he made a Stroke**. Does this mean he did the right thing? **Absolutely not. ** This belongs to the "**making mistakes + making money**" box in the four quadrants of F7 - the **most dangerous box**: the money earned by luck will make him **mistakenly believe that "catching flying knives + carrying orders" is effective**, and thus repeat this bad habit, **sooner or later the market will take back the capital and profits**. Therefore, what is evaluated is always the process, not the single result.

**2. Failure cases only teach lessons, not humiliation. **
The failure of the review is not to regret "Why was I so stupid at the time", nor to deny myself. Its only purpose is to calmly find out "what went wrong and how to correct it next time", and turn this loss into a stroke of valuable "tuition". **Humiliating yourself** will only lead to emotional internal friction and even trigger revenge transactions (F5), which will not help improve. The same applies to other people's failure cases - we analyze the lessons and don't laugh at the parties involved.

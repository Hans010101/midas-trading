# RSI Indicator: Overbought and Oversold

> Midas Academy · Phase 2 · Module 6 Classic Technical Indicators

**Quick Overview**

- **RSI ranges from 0–100**, reflecting the comparison of recent bullish and bearish strength; **50 is the centerline**, with strength above indicating bullish dominance and below indicating bearish dominance.
- **Overbought zones are often referenced at >70, and oversold zones at <30** (thresholds are not unique).
- **Overbought ≠ immediate drop, Oversold ≠ immediate rise**: In strong trends, RSI can **dull** (stagnate) and remain at extreme values for a long time; overbought and oversold are **reference zones, not reversal signals**.
- RSI also has a **divergence** application (price direction opposite to RSI), with the same principle as MACD divergence; it does not predict timing and may fail, serving only as a reference.

## I. Concept Introduction: Measuring Bullish/Bearish Strength with a 0–100 Number

The MACD and Bollinger Bands learned previously are based on price moving averages and volatility. **RSI (Relative Strength Index)** takes a different angle: it compares the "strength of upward movement" and "strength of downward movement" over a period of time, **condensing this into a value between 0 and 100**, allowing you to instantly see which side is dominating recently.

RSI is one of the most common "oscillators." This section covers its value meaning, overbought/oversold zones, and a critical cognitive misunderstanding.

## II. What is RSI: 0–100, 50 as the Centerline

RSI values always stay within **0 to 100**. Its calculation is simply the comparison of **average upward amplitude and average downward amplitude over a period of time** (commonly 14 periods)—the stronger the upward strength, the higher the RSI; the stronger the downward strength, the lower the RSI.

To understand RSI, grasp these key benchmarks:

- **50 (Centerline)**: The dividing line where bullish and bearish forces are roughly balanced. RSI above 50 indicates recent upward strength is relatively dominant; below 50 indicates downward strength is relatively dominant.
- **Closer to 100**: Upward strength is overwhelming.
- **Closer to 0**: Downward strength is overwhelming.

## III. Overbought and Oversold: Two "Reference Zones"

The most well-known use of RSI is observing two zones:

- **Overbought Zone**: RSI enters a **high** zone (**often referenced at 70, >70**). This means recent upward strength is strong, the price has risen significantly, and the move is relatively "sharp."
- **Oversold Zone**: RSI enters a **low** zone (**often referenced at 30, <30**). This means recent downward strength is strong, and the price has fallen significantly.

(Note: 70 / 30 are the most common reference thresholds, but not the only standard; some also use 80 / 20, which can be adjusted based on the asset and personal preference.)

## IV. The Most Critical Insight: Overbought ≠ Immediate Drop, Oversold ≠ Immediate Rise

This is the most easily misused aspect of RSI and must be clearly explained—

Many beginners see "Overbought" and think "it's going to drop," or see "Oversold" and think "it's going to rise," treating it as a "buy/sell signal for reversal." **This is a dangerous misunderstanding.**

The truth is: **Overbought and oversold are "reference zones," not "reversal signals."**

- In a **strong uptrend**, RSI may **remain in the overbought zone (>70) for a long time** while the price continues to rise—this phenomenon is called **dulling** (the indicator becomes "ineffective" after entering extreme values and no longer effectively signals a reversal). At this point, "overbought" absolutely does not mean an immediate drop.
- Similarly, in a strong downtrend, RSI may **stay in the oversold zone for a long time** while the price continues to fall.

Therefore, the correct understanding is: Overbought tells you "the uptrend is strong and the price is high, worth watching," **but you cannot conclude it will reverse based on this, nor can you predict when it will reverse**. Using overbought and oversold as "guaranteed reversal signals" often leads to **repeated losses** in strong trends. It is merely a reference; you must view it in conjunction with the trend and other information.

## V. RSI Divergence Application

Just like MACD, RSI can also observe **divergence** (the principle is exactly the same as the MACD divergence in B6):

- **RSI Top Divergence**: Price creates a new high (higher high), but the RSI high actually drops → upward momentum is weakening.
- **RSI Bottom Divergence**: Price creates a new low (lower low), but the RSI low actually rises → downward momentum is weakening.

It is also a reference clue indicating "momentum exhaustion and a possible trend shift." But like all divergences: **it does not predict an exact timing and may dull or fail**, serving only as a reference to heighten vigilance. It must be combined with other information and risk management and does not constitute any buy or sell advice.

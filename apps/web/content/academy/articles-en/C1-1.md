# Segment Division and Two Types of Break

> Midas Academy · Chan Theory Advanced · Structure Advanced

In the beginner stage, we mentioned: A Segment must consist of at least three consecutive Strokes, and they must overlap. However, in real combat, what truly torments traders is not "what is a Segment," but rather——**Has this segment actually finished? Where does it end?** When a reverse Stroke appears, is the Segment destroyed, or is it just a normal fluctuation within the Segment?

Chan Theory solves this problem not by relying on "it feels about right," but by a set of tools that are **precise enough to be executed mechanically**: **Feature Sequence + Two Types of Destruction**. In this article, we will explain it thoroughly. This is widely considered the most convoluted part of Chan Theory structure, so please read slowly.

**Article Overview**

- **Segment Basics**: At least **three consecutive Strokes**, alternating directions, and **overlap between the first and third Stroke**.
- **Feature Sequence**: Elements are the **Strokes opposite to the Segment direction** (take downward Strokes for an upward Segment); each element has high and low points; after **non-inclusion processing**, it becomes a **Standard Feature Sequence** (processing is only done on the same side of the Fractal).
- The appearance of a **Fractal in the Feature Sequence** is a prerequisite for the Segment to potentially end; whether it truly ends depends on **whether there is a gap between the 1st and 2nd elements of the Fractal**.
- **Type 1 Destruction (No Gap)**: The Segment ends immediately when the Fractal is established; the Segment endpoint is at the Fractal's extreme value (equivalent to "Stroke Destruction").
- **Type 2 Destruction (With Gap)**: Confirmation requires waiting for the **reverse Feature Sequence** to also form a Fractal starting from the extreme value point; the second Fractal no longer checks for gaps and does not need to close the gap.
- The distinction into two types is to **filter false endings and avoid misjudging a small level as a large level**; Segment division is the part of Chan Theory with the most disagreement; understand it within the framework and do not mechanically apply it.

## I. Nail Down the Basic Definition of a Segment First

Before advancing, solidify the foundation. A standard Segment must simultaneously satisfy:

- **Direction**: Composed of alternating Strokes (Upward Segment = Up Stroke → Down Stroke → Up Stroke... starting and ending with an Up Stroke);
- **Quantity**: At least **three consecutive Strokes**;
- **Overlap**: **The first and third Strokes must have a price overlap interval** (This is the hard condition for a Segment to exist—without overlap, three Strokes cannot form a Segment).

Remember this "overlap" requirement; it is the prerequisite for understanding "gaps" later.

## II. Feature Sequence: "Translating" a Segment into a Readable Sequence

To determine if a Segment has ended, Chan Theory does not look directly at the Segment itself, but first "translates" it into a **Feature Sequence**.

**Core Rule (Must remember the direction): The Feature Sequence is composed of "Strokes opposite to the Segment direction."**

- For an **Upward Segment**, take all **Downward Strokes** within it as the elements of the Feature Sequence;
- For a **Downward Segment**, take all **Upward Strokes** within it as the elements of the Feature Sequence.

Why take the opposite direction? Because for an upward trend to end, it relies on "downward power" gradually gaining the upper hand—and Downward Strokes are precisely the "footprints" of this reverse power. By lining up these reverse Strokes and observing their changes, you can capture the turning point of the Segment's power.

Each Feature Sequence element has its own **high and low points** (the highest and lowest prices of that Stroke). We will use these highs and lows for comparison later.

## III. Standard Feature Sequence: Feature Sequences Also Require "Inclusion Processing"

There is a step that is easily skipped but is very critical.

Chan Theory states: Treat **every element of the Feature Sequence as a single K-line**. Since they are K-lines, adjacent elements may have an **inclusion relationship**—at this point, perform **non-inclusion processing** on it (merging by taking the higher high for upward and lower low for downward, just like processing K-line inclusion). The Feature Sequence processed and free of inclusion relationships is called a **Standard Feature Sequence**.

Two practical reminders:

1. **Perform inclusion processing only on the "same Feature Sequence".** That is, only merge elements on the same side of the Fractal; elements on the other side of the Fractal belong to another Feature Sequence and do not participate in the inclusion processing on this side.
2. Empirically, **you can be less concerned with inclusion when there is no gap, but must strictly perform inclusion processing when there is a gap** (the reason is explained below).

## IV. Fractal of the Feature Sequence = Signal for Segment Potential End

Once the Feature Sequence (after standardization) is lined up, you can look for **Fractals** on it—just like finding Fractals on K-lines:

- For an **Upward Segment** (Feature Sequence composed of Downward Strokes), we look for the **Top Fractal** of the Feature Sequence (the middle element's high point is the highest), which corresponds to "the rise may have reached the top";
- For a **Downward Segment**, look for the **Bottom Fractal** of the Feature Sequence.

**The appearance of a Fractal in the Feature Sequence is a prerequisite condition for the Segment to potentially end.** But note—it is a "prerequisite condition," not "immediate end." Whether it counts as an end depends on this critical next step: **Whether there is a "gap" between the first and second elements of the Fractal.** It is precisely this "whether there is a gap" that divides Segment destruction into two situations.

## V. Type 1 Destruction (No Gap): Segment Ends as Soon as the Fractal is Established

**The first situation: There is no Feature Sequence gap between the first and second elements of the Feature Sequence Fractal (meaning these two elements overlap).**

This situation is the cleanest: **As long as this Standard Feature Sequence Fractal is established, the Segment ends at the Fractal's extreme value**—the Top Fractal ends at its highest point, the Bottom Fractal ends at its lowest point, and that point is the Segment's endpoint.

Why is it so clean? Because the overlap and lack of a gap between the first and second elements mean the reverse power has taken over "continuously and solidly"—this essentially constitutes effective destruction of the original Segment (in Chan Theory, this situation is also valid as "Stroke Destruction"). You can remember one sentence: **In the absence of a gap, any three Strokes meeting the conditions constitute destruction of the previous Segment.**

## VI. Type 2 Destruction (With Gap): Wait for Reverse Feature Sequence Confirmation

**The second situation: There is a Feature Sequence gap between the first and second elements of the Feature Sequence Fractal (no overlap between the two elements, leaving a gap).**

In this situation, **you cannot jump to a conclusion immediately**. Just seeing a Fractal with a gap is not enough to confirm the Segment has ended. You must wait one more step:

> Starting from the extreme value point of this Fractal (highest point of Top Fractal / lowest point of Bottom Fractal), the segment moving in the opposite direction must **also form a Fractal in its own Feature Sequence**—only then is the original Segment confirmed to have ended at that extreme value point.

In other words, when there is a gap, you must use the **"Fractal of the subsequent reverse Feature Sequence"** to confirm the previous Fractal. Two supplementary points:

- This second (reverse) Feature Sequence Fractal **does not necessarily need to close the gap mentioned earlier**;
- Furthermore, the second Fractal **no longer distinguishes "whether there is a gap"**—as long as it forms a Fractal, it counts as confirmation.

## VII. Why Must We Distinguish Between These Two Types?

You might ask: Why go through all this trouble? Can't we use a unified standard?

Chan Theory distinguishes between two types mainly to **avoid uncertain situations where a "small level suddenly becomes a large level" at the Segment level**. The "gap" here actually reflects **momentum**—a reverse Fractal with a gap may be just a fierce but brief counterattack and may not truly destroy the original Segment; therefore, we must wait one more step for reverse confirmation to filter out "false endings." In the case of no gap, the reverse power takes over continuously, so the uncertainty is low and it can be confirmed directly.

Practically, two sentences are worth remembering: **"Count as a Segment within a Segment, count as a Stroke within a Stroke"**—a gap appearing inside a Stroke, following the trend, does not count as a Segment-level gap; only a gap that goes against the trend and is sudden needs to be treated as a Segment-level gap.

> ⚠️ Must be stated: Segment division (especially Type 2 Destruction and Feature Sequence inclusion processing) is the part of Chan Theory that is **most prone to disagreement and tests one's skill the most**; different people often have different handling on boundary cases. This article provides the standard official stance of the original Chan Theory (Segment division standards) to be understood as an **analytical framework**; when encountering complex real trading, it is recommended to repeatedly study the original text and do not mechanically apply it. Segment division itself also has "tolerance"—many Segments in a trend are not critical, and the Segments that truly require scrutiny are those at key turning points.

## VIII. Key Points of This Article

- **Segment Basics:** **At least three consecutive Strokes, alternating directions, and overlap between the first and third Stroke**.
- **Feature Sequence:** Select the Strokes **opposite** to the Segment direction as elements (select downward Strokes for an upward Segment); each element has high and low points; after **non-inclusion processing**, they become the **Standard Feature Sequence** (processing is performed only on the same side of the Fractal).
- The appearance of a **Fractal in the Feature Sequence** is a prerequisite for the Segment to potentially end; whether it truly ends depends on whether there is a **gap between the 1st and 2nd elements of the Fractal**.
- **First Type of Destruction (No Gap):** The Segment ends immediately upon the establishment of the Fractal; the Segment's endpoint is at the extreme value of the Fractal (equivalent to "Stroke Destruction").
- **Second Type of Destruction (With Gap):** It must be confirmed only after the **reverse Feature Sequence** also forms a Fractal starting from the extreme value; the second Fractal no longer checks for gaps and does not need to close the gap.
- Distinguishing these two types is to **filter false endings and avoid misjudging a small level as a large level**; Segment division is the biggest area of contention in Chan Theory; understand it according to the framework and do not apply it mechanically.

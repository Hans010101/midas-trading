# Moving Strategies to Futures: What Leverage Changes First

> Midas Academy · Level 5 · Module 16 Trading Strategy Types (Contract Practice)

**Quick overview of this article**

- **Contracts are not "more advanced spot", but tools with completely different risk structures**; doing it with the mentality of "contract = advanced spot" is the starting point for failure.
- Leverage brings four essential changes: **two-way amplification of profits and losses, introduction of Liquidation (the principal may return to zero), introduction of capital costs, and introduction of long and short bidirectional**.
- When the same strategy is applied to the contract, **the fault tolerance space is compressed and the cost of errors is magnified** - a normal callback may make you Stop Loss/Liquidation out of the game.
- The background of the entire contract actual series: **Leverage only magnifies the results but does not improve the odds of winning, Liquidation is a hard constraint, capital fees are ongoing costs, profits are not guaranteed and the risk is much higher than spot**.
- ⚠️ Red line: Leverage is by no means beautifying (amplifying profits will also magnify losses, possibly Liquidation); the contract** does not guarantee profits and needs to be coordinated with risk management. What was valid in the past does not mean it will be valid in the future**; entry and exit are logical indications, not buying and selling signals.

## 1. Concept introduction: Don’t treat contracts as “spots with added leverage”

After learning the previous common trading strategies (F11-F18: trend following, breakthrough, mean reversion, grid...), many people will naturally think: **"Can't we just move these strategies to crypto contracts?"**

The idea itself is correct - strategies can indeed be applied to contracts. But copying it directly is often dangerous. Because the contract introduces something that completely changes the entire risk structure: **Leverage**.

The most common misunderstanding is: thinking that "making trends and breakthroughs in contracts is just like spot trading, and adding leverage to amplify profits." The result? For the same strategy, if you do it in spot trading, you may only make a small loss, but if you do it in contract, Liquidation will be eliminated and the principal will return to zero.

This article is the final chapter of the entire "Contract Practice" series. It needs to make one thing clear: **When you put the strategy into a contract, what does leverage change first - it changes the risk structure into a completely different thing. ** Think this through clearly so that each subsequent article will have the correct premise.

## 2. The contract is not a "more advanced spot"

Let’s first correct a fundamental understanding:

**Contract is not a "more advanced and powerful spot", but a tool with a completely different risk structure from spot. **

Many people subconsciously regard the contract as "Spot Plus" - it seems to be an upgraded version of the spot, a version only used by experts. This understanding is very dangerous. The correct understanding is:

- Spot and contract are two tools with different risk structures;
- The contract is not "better", but "the risk is amplified and a new risk (Liquidation) is introduced";
- Using a contract means that you accept a completely different and much stricter set of risk rules. **

Going into contracts with the mentality of "contract = advanced spot" is the starting point for many people to stumble.

## 3. What has leverage changed: four essential changes

Specifically, when the strategy is moved from spot to contract, leverage brings four essential changes.

### Change 1: Two-way amplification of profits and losses

This is the most direct. Leverage allows you to control much larger positions with a smaller margin (review A5, E1). With N times leverage, profits and losses are magnified approximately N times.

But the key lies in the words "**two-way**":

> ⚠️ While leverage magnifies profits, it also magnifies losses in the same way**. With 10 times leverage, if the price fluctuates 1% in your favor, you earn about 10%; but if the price fluctuates 1% in the opposite direction, you also lose about 10%. **Any statement that only talks about "magnifying profits" without talking about "magnifying losses equally" is dangerously misleading. **

### Change 2: Introducing "Liquidation" - the principal may return to zero

This is the most essential new risk in the contract, which is not found in the spot market at all.

- **Spot**: If the price falls, you just have a **floating loss**. As long as you don't sell it, you can theoretically hold it and wait for it to come back (the worst case is that the assets return to zero, but that is an extreme situation).
- **Contract**: When the price reverses to a certain extent and the loss is close to your margin, it will be **Forced Liquidation (Liquidation)**, and the margin will suffer a large loss or even return to zero (review E4). You don't have the option of "holding it and waiting for it to come back" - the market won't give you this opportunity.

Liquidation is the sword hanging over the head of every contract position. It changes "floating losses can wait" to "forced exit when losses reach a certain level."

### Change 3: Introducing capital fee costs

To hold a Perpetual Contract, you have to pay fees regularly according to the Funding Rate (Review E2: Long positions with positive rates pay short positions, short positions with negative rates pay long positions, which are transfer fees between long and short positions, non-platform handling fees).

- Spot: buy and hold, there is no such ongoing cost.
- Contract: If you hold a direction that requires payment for a long time, the capital fee will continue to erode your income - this is a holding cost that does not exist in Stroke spot but is easily ignored.

### Change 4: Introducing long and short bi-directional

The contract can conveniently **Go Short**, which means that the strategy can be used in both directions (spots can generally only go Long).

This is the flexibility of the contract - there are opportunities for both ups and downs. But be clear: **Go Short’s flexibility, behind it is symmetrical risk**. Go Short also loses money when it is wrong (price rises), and theoretically there is no upper limit to price rise (review of Group A Go Short risks). Two-way is not "one more way to make a sure profit", but "one more direction of risk exposure".

## 4. How does the risk change with the same strategy?

Putting these four changes into a specific strategy will make the experience more direct. Take **Trend Tracking** (F12) as an example:

- **In the spot**: You bought with the trend, but you were wrong, and the price corrected - you just suffered a floating loss. You can stop the loss as planned, or (if you are bullish in the long term) wait for the trend to start again. **Large fault tolerance space. **
- **In the contract**: You bought with the trend and added leverage, but you also misread it and the price fell back - but the leverage magnified the floating loss. This "normal callback" may hit your Stop Loss or even direct Liquidation** before the trend really unfolds. **The fault tolerance space is greatly compressed. **

The same strategy and the same judgment, because of the existence of leverage in the contract, the error tolerance space is compressed and the cost of errors is magnified. This is the price that must be recognized first when "moving the strategy into the contract" (the next article F20 will specifically talk about the "opening price-Stop Loss price-Liquidation price" relationship between trend/breakthrough in the contract).

## 5. This article sets the tone: establishes rules for the entire contract combat series

As the main gateway to the actual contract, this article must establish several "rules" that will be followed throughout the follow-up:

1. **Leverage magnifies "results", not "probability of winning". ** It amplifies both right and wrong outcomes, but ** does not increase your probability of being right **. Leverage cannot make a money-losing strategy profitable, it will only make it lose money faster.
2. **Liquidation is the "hard constraint" of the contract. ** Any contract strategy must consider "whether there will be Liquidation and where the Liquidation price will be" as the first priority (see F20 and F23 for details).
3. **The capital fee is an ongoing cost. ** The profit and loss of a contract position not only depends on the price, but also includes funding fees.
4. **Contracts do not guarantee profits, and the risks are much higher than spot prices. ** This is the unchanging base color throughout the series.

Each subsequent article (trend breakthrough, arbitrage, hedging, position management, error trap) is based on this premise.

# Market Order vs. Limit Order: How to Place Orders

> **Midas Academy · Phase 1 · Module 4: Fundamentals of Trading**

**Quick Overview**

- There are two common ways to place orders: **Market Orders** and **Limit Orders**. The core difference lies in prioritizing "timeliness" or "price."
- **Market Order**: Executed immediately at the current market price. Fast but price is not fully controllable (slippage may occur).
- **Limit Order**: Placed at a specified price; execution occurs only when the price reaches that level. Price is controllable but execution is not guaranteed.
- Both have trade-offs; **there is no absolute superiority**. The choice depends on whether you prioritize execution speed or execution price.

## I. Concept Introduction: Buying is Not One-Size-Fits-All

When you decide to buy or sell, you must use an "order" to tell the market your intent. However, there is more than one way to place an order—the two most basic and common are **Market Orders** and **Limit Orders**.

The difference between them comes down to answering this question: **Do you prioritize "immediate execution," or do you prioritize "execution price"?** These two goals are often mutually exclusive, leading to two different types of orders. In this section, we will objectively explain the mechanisms of both. As for how to choose specifically, it depends on your own purpose and scenario.

## II. Market Order: Immediate Execution at Current Market Price

A **Market Order** means: **No price is specified; you buy or sell directly at the best available price in the market immediately.**

Its characteristics can be summarized in one sentence: **Sacrifice "price certainty" to gain "execution timeliness."**

- **Pros**: Fast execution. As long as there is a counterparty in the market, it executes almost instantly; no waiting required.
- **Cons**: Price is not fully controllable. At the exact moment you place the order, you will roughly execute at the current market price. When prices fluctuate violently, or liquidity is low (insufficient buy/sell depth), the actual execution price may deviate from the price you see—this deviation is called **slippage**.

To use an analogy: A market order is like saying at the vegetable market, "I want this vegetable right now, calculate the price based on the current market rate." It is decisive, but the final unit price is subject to the prevailing market conditions.

## III. Limit Order: Placed at a Specified Price, Waiting for Price to Reach It

A **Limit Order** means: **You specify a price to place an order; execution occurs only when the market price reaches (or is better than) that price.**

Its characteristics are exactly the opposite of a market order: **Sacrifice "execution timeliness" to gain "price certainty."**

- **Pros**: Price is controllable. The execution price will not be worse than your limit price—when buying, you won't pay more than your limit; when selling, you won't receive less than your limit.
- **Cons**: Execution is not guaranteed. If the price never reaches your specified level, the order will remain pending and may not execute, causing you to miss the opportunity.

Example: Assume the current price is 100, and you want to buy cheaper. You place a "Buy at 98" limit order. It will only execute when the price drops to 98 (or lower); if the price rises instead and never touches 98, you won't be able to buy. (The selling direction is the mirror image: you can place a sell limit order above the current price, waiting for the price to rise to execute.)

## IV. Comparison and Suitable Scenarios

When placed side by side, the differences are clear:

| Dimension | Market Order | Limit Order |
|---|---|---|
| Execution Speed | Fast, immediate execution | Uncertain, requires waiting for price to reach level |
| Price Control | No, executes at current market price | Yes, at specified price or better |
| Guaranteed Execution | Generally executes | May not execute |
| Main Risk | Potential slippage | Potential missed opportunity or order not filling |

From the perspective of applicable scenarios (this is an objective description of mechanisms, not operational advice):

- When a trader **prioritizes "guaranteed execution"** and wants to enter or exit immediately, the timeliness of a market order meets this need.
- When a trader **prioritizes "execution price"**, is willing to wait for an ideal price level, and accepts the possibility of non-execution, the price control of a limit order meets this need.

No type is "better"—they simply serve different priorities. By understanding what each one "gives up" and "gains," you can choose based on your own objectives.

# Using Leverage Appropriately vs. Excessively

> Midas Academy · C2 contract case library (full virtual / indicating market conditions)

Old traders often say: "**Many people lose not in direction, but in leverage.**" In this article, we use a fictitious case to let two people with exactly the same views use different leverage in the same market to see how far the ending can be.

> ⚠️ This article is **fictitious / indicative market conditions and fictitious operations** for teaching purposes. All numbers are indicative. It does not name any real targets, does not predict the real market, and does not constitute buying or selling advice. Contracts are high-risk instruments. This article presents the risks truthfully and does not encourage high leverage.

## 1. Scene setting

- A **symbol of the market**: a fictitious contract, the price starts from **100**, first **normally adjusts to 96**, and then rises all the way to **115**.
- Two traders **A and B**: have exactly the same views - both are bullish, both enter the market at **100**, both plan to wait for an increase, and have similar Stop Loss ideas.
- The only difference: **A uses 3x leverage and B uses 50x leverage. **

The margin for both people is assumed to be 1,000 yuan (A’s exposure is 3,000, B’s exposure is 50,000). Note - their ** direction judgment is the same, and the final direction is also correct ** (the price did rise to 115).

## 2. Operation process

**A (3x leverage):**
- Go Long 100, under 3 times leverage, the **Liquidation price is approximately 100×(1−1/3) ≈ 67** (far away from the position price);
- When the price retraced to 96 - still far away from the Liquidation price of 67, A's position was **unscathed** and could be held;
- The price subsequently rose to 115, and A successfully benefited from this increase.

**B (50x leverage):**
- Same Go Long 100, but under 50 times leverage, the **Liquidation price is approximately 100×(1−1/50) = 98** (including maintenance margin and handling fees, it will actually be earlier, such as 98.x);
- When the price retraces to 96 - **has fallen below B's Liquidation price of 98**! B’s position was directly eliminated by Forced Liquidation and Liquidation;
- When the price rose to 115, B was no longer on the market - he was looking in the right direction, but he didn't make any money and lost all his deposit. **

## 3. Dismantling of key nodes

**Node 1: That "normal callback to 96" was a watershed moment. **
A pullback to 96 is a normal fluctuation for this uptrend (there is a pullback in the trend). But **the same callback has two fates for the two levers**: A's Liquidation price of 67 is far away, so the callback cannot affect him; B's Liquidation price of 98 is too close, and the callback directly kicks him out.

**Node 2: The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the position price. **
This is the hard truth taught by Group E - **Liquidation price ≈ opening price × (1−1/leverage), long orders are below the opening price; the higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the opening price** (only 2% difference at 50 times). A 2% fluctuation may only take a few minutes in the contract market. High leverage means compressing one's "fault tolerance space" to almost zero.

**Node 3: Maintenance margin makes actual Liquidation earlier. **
The theoretical Liquidation price of B is 98, but including the maintenance margin rate and handling fees, **the actual liquidation is often triggered a little earlier than 98** - under high leverage, this difference is enough to determine life or death.

## 4. Key points of review

The most disturbing part of this case is that **B’s direction judgment was completely correct, but he was eliminated because of excessive use of leverage. **

- **The lever does not change whether your judgment of the direction is right or wrong, it only changes whether you can hold it or not. ** Making the right judgment does not mean making money - you still have to "live" until the market price materializes.
- **High leverage compresses fault tolerance**: A normal callback in the trend is enough for you to liquidate under high leverage. You think that adding leverage is "amplifying profits", but the result is "being eliminated early in normal fluctuations".
- **The other side of "magnifying profits" is always "equally magnifying losses, possibly Liquidation"** - B is a living example.
- Using appropriate leverage (low leverage) is to leave room for error in judgment; using excessive leverage (high leverage) is to leave yourself to luck.

One sentence to end: if the direction is right, you may not win; if the leverage is wrong, you will most likely lose. ** Surviving a long time in a contract is often not because of how accurate your predictions are, but because you don't use excessive leverage**.

## 5. Main points of this article

- Judgment of the same market and the same direction, **low leverage (A) can hold on safely, high leverage (B) Liquidation will be out during a normal callback** - B is in the right direction but loses money.
- **Leverage does not change the direction of judgment, it only changes "whether it can be held"**; the right judgment ≠ makes money, and you have to live until the market cashes out.
- **The higher the leverage, the closer the Liquidation price is to the opening price (≈100÷N%, long orders are at the bottom)**, and the maintenance of margin will actually occur earlier; high leverage reduces the fault tolerance space to almost zero.
- ⚠️ The entire process is virtual/signaled; magnifying profits will also magnify losses, and liquidation is possible; high leverage is not encouraged, does not constitute advice, and does not predict.

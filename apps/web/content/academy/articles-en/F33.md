## 1. Concept introduction: Once you have a strategic idea, how do you test it?

F18 talked about the concept of backtesting and the most important red line - **The validity of backtesting does not mean that it will be valid in the future**. This article puts it into practice: in Midas's **strategy laboratory**, how to turn a strategic idea into a testable rule step by step, backtest it, understand the indicators, and then iteratively polish it.

The pain point is very common: you have a strategic idea ("I think this should work"), but you don't know how to verify whether it is reliable or not - you can't just try it with real money. The strategy research room is a place used for low-cost testing.

## 2. Practical process: Idea → Rules → Backtesting → Reading indicators → Iteration

Polishing a strategy in the strategy research room is roughly the following process (continue from F18):

**Step one: Idea → Regularization. **
A vague idea (such as "buy if it breaks" or "copy if it oversells") cannot be tested** and must first be translated into clear, quantifiable, and unambiguous rules**:

- **Entry Conditions**: Specific signals/prices before entering;
- **Exit Conditions**: Where is the Stop Loss and where is the Take Profit (or move the Stop Loss rules);
- **Position Rules**: How much to invest and how much risk to take for each Stroke (receive F4).

This step is the prerequisite for backtesting - the clearer the rules, the more they can be objectively tested; vague "feelings" cannot be backtested. ** And this step is valuable in itself: many ideas that "sound good" reveal themselves to be unclear and contradictory when they are forced to be written down into precise rules.

![F33 Picture 1](/academy-img/F33-配图1.png)

**Step 2: Backtest in the Strategy Lab. **
Run the rules on historical data, and the Strategy Research Office will give you a **Net Value Curve** and a series of **Performance Indicators**. (The entire process is virtual, used for analysis and inspection.)

**Step 3: Read the indicators (then F18). **
Focus on these, and look at them comprehensively:

- **Cumulative income**: Total income - **Don't just look at this one**;
- **Maximum retracement**: The maximum drop from the highest point to the lowest point, measuring "how much you lose at the most difficult time", is a key indicator of **risk**. A strategy with too large a retracement may make you unable to hold on and give up midway through the actual offer;
- **Winning rate**: Proportion of profitable transactions (recall F3: **High winning rate ≠ Making money**);
- **Profit and loss ratio**: average profit ÷ average loss (recall F3: it must be combined with the winning rate).

**Looking at it comprehensively, we should pay special attention to "maximum drawdown" and "profit and loss ratio", rather than just focusing on cumulative returns. ** A strategy that can be executed in the long term is often not the one with the highest return, but the one with a balance between return and drawdown that allows you to sleep well.

![F33 with picture 2](/academy-img/F33-配图2.png)

**Step 4: Iterate. **
Based on the backtest results, **understand the characteristics of the strategy** (how big is its retracement, under what market conditions does it make money, and under what market conditions does it lose money), find obvious problems, **adjust the rules**, and then backtest it. And so on.

## 3. ★Correct usage of backtesting (red line core)

This must be emphasized repeatedly - what exactly is backtesting used for? **

- **Backtesting is a tool used to "eliminate obviously unreliable rules and understand the characteristics of strategies." ** If a rule cannot even outrun history, then there is a high probability that it does not need to be considered; through backtesting, you can also understand the "probably what temper" this strategy is like.
- ★**Backtesting is not used to "prove that this strategy can make money in the future". ** **Backtest valid ≠ valid in the future. ** Backtesting reflects the past, while you are trading the future - there is no inevitable bridge between the two.

In a word: **Backtesting can help you "eliminate bad ones and understand features", but it cannot "guarantee good future earnings". **

## 4. Traps of backtesting (must be wary)

Backtesting is the easiest way to deceive people. Beware of several traps (continue F18):

**1. Overfitting (curve fitting). **
If you repeatedly adjust parameters until the backtest results are "very beautiful" - it is likely that you just overfit the rules to this historical data (remembering the "noise" of history rather than the rules). This kind of "perfect backtest" will often fail immediately in the future. **The more beautiful and unrealistic the backtest is, the more suspicious it is of overfitting. **

**2. Survivor bias. **
If you only backtest on the varieties/data that are "still alive" and ignore those that have been delisted and returned to zero, you will **systematically overestimate** the performance - because you only count the "survivors".

**3. History does not simply repeat itself. **
Markets change (participants, liquidity, policies, algorithms). **What worked in the past does not mean it will work in the future** (echoing F17).

## 5. After backtesting: virtual real disk verification

The backtest looks at "history", and you need to use **virtual transaction** to verify a period forward (continue F18, F35):

- In the **virtual trading** of Midas, follow the rules and run for a while in a market that is **forward and close to the real**, to see how it performs in the "**unseen market**" and **whether you can strictly implement it** (execution problems are often more fatal than rule problems, go to F6).
- **Backtest to look at historical performance, virtual look at forward execution**—the combination of the two is relatively complete.
